The "2017 Transitional Protection" for Your Indian Provident Fund Is Not What the Top-Ranking Guides Say It Is

This is the first UK-vertical page in this series: the reader is a UK tax resident with an Indian Employees' Provident Fund balance, and the sources are UK statute and HMRC's own manuals, not the IRS materials the first thirteen parts are built on. The #1-ranking guide for this topic says pre-6-April-2017 PF value is shielded by "transitional protection HMRC introduced." Finance Act 2017 Schedule 3, read in full, says close to the opposite: the headline 2017 change removed a relief, and the pre-2017 deduction that does exist is narrow, formula-gated, and — at its most generous step — conditional on a statutory scheme-classification question that no ranking guide found even asks.

New vertical: UK · Sources read in full · Finance Act 2017, Schedule 3 (legislation.gov.uk) · HMRC Employment Income Manual EIM75550 & EIM75500 · HMRC Pensions Tax Manual PTM112200 & PTM021000 · three ranking competitor guides, quoted verbatim below

Roughly 1.86 million people of Indian origin live in England and Wales (2021 Census). A large share of the recent arrivals — intra-company transferees, Skilled Worker visa holders, students who stayed — left behind an EPF account that keeps compounding in India. When that money is finally withdrawn as a lump sum by someone who is by then UK tax resident, India will usually not tax it, and the UK usually will. Every ranking UK guide agrees on that much. Where they diverge from the statute is on how much of it the UK taxes, and why.

1. What the top-ranking guide claims

CoreAdviz Accountants — a real UK practice whose article "Indian Provident Fund (PF) Tax in the UK | NRI Tax Guide" (dated 2 February 2026) ranks #1 on Google for this topic's core queries — states, quoted verbatim:

"✅ Key Date: 6 April 2017 — If your Indian PF has accrued value before you became a UK resident or before 6 April 2017, the UK may not tax that portion of the fund. This is part of the 'transitional protection' HMRC introduced to avoid unfair taxation of previously earned foreign pension rights."

Two claims are packed in there: that the 2017 change was a taxpayer-favourable protection HMRC introduced, and that the protected amount is measured by either of two dates — 6 April 2017 or the date you became UK resident. The second date test appears nowhere in the legislation. The first claim inverts what the 2017 Act's most prominent change actually did.

What Finance Act 2017 Schedule 3 actually did first

The headline 2017 change removed a relief. It did not create one.

Schedule 3, Part 2 of the Finance Act 2017 carries its own heading, quoted verbatim from legislation.gov.uk:

"Part 2 — Income tax on pension income — UK residents to be taxed on 100%, not 90%, of foreign pension income

2(1) Omit section 575(2) of ITEPA 2003 (foreign pensions received by UK residents: taxable amount is 90% of actual amount)."

Before 6 April 2017, a UK resident receiving foreign pension income was taxed on 90% of it — a standing 10% statutory deduction under ITEPA 2003 ss.575(2), 613(3) and 635(3). Schedule 3 deleted all three. Describing the 2017 package as "transitional protection… introduced to avoid unfair taxation" gets the direction of the flagship change backwards: it made foreign pension income more taxable, not less.

2. The pre-2017 deduction that does exist is a formula, not a shield

The provision the "6 April 2017" folklore is actually about is Schedule 3, Part 3 ("Lump sums for UK residents from foreign pension schemes"), which inserted a new section 574A into ITEPA 2003. It works as a three-step reduction from a default of full taxability. Step 2 — the pre-2017 step — splits into two different regimes depending on what kind of scheme paid the lump sum, quoted verbatim:

"Step 2 — Where the lump sum is paid under a pension scheme that was an employer-financed retirement benefits scheme immediately before 6 April 2017, deduct so much of the lump sum left after Step 1 as is deductible in accordance with subsection (6). Where the lump sum is paid otherwise than under such a scheme, deduct so much of the lump sum left after Step 1 as is paid in respect of the value immediately before 6 April 2017 of rights, accrued by then, specifically to receive benefits by way of lump sum payments."

If the scheme was an "employer-financed retirement benefits scheme" (EFRBS) immediately before 6 April 2017 — a classification never established for Indian EPF by any source found — the deductible amount is only the full pre-2017 value if one of three foreign-service composition tests in subsection (6)(f) is met, quoted verbatim:

"(i) at least 75% of pre-6 April 2017 reckonable service is made up of foreign service, or (ii) the period of pre-6 April 2017 reckonable service exceeds 10 years and the whole of the last 10 years of that period is made up of foreign service, or (iii) the period of pre-6 April 2017 reckonable service exceeds 20 years and at least 50% of that period, including any 10 of the last 20 years, is made up of foreign service;"

Fail all three, and subsection (6)(g) gives only "the appropriate fraction" of the pre-2017 value — F/R, pre-2017 foreign service over total pre-2017 reckonable service. If the scheme was not an EFRBS, the second sentence of Step 2 applies instead: the deduction reaches only rights accrued before 6 April 2017 specifically to receive lump sum benefits, valued at that date. For someone who has kept an EPF account compounding since 2017, everything credited after 5 April 2017 — every year of 8%-plus interest — sits outside the deduction on either route. HMRC's own manual states the default bluntly (EIM75550, quoted verbatim):

"The starting point is that 100% of the lump sum payment will be taxable, subject to the 3 steps at section 574A(3) under which a reduction of the taxable amount of the lump sum may be available."

That is HMRC's own framing of the regime the #1 guide describes as protection: 100% taxable, minus what you can prove into a deduction.

3. The two guides that cite the right section still skip its biggest gate

Two other ranking competitors are materially better than CoreAdviz — and still diverge from the statute at the same place. Roy Accountants ("Indian Provident fund and UK taxation," 9 May 2023, read in full) hedges correctly ("value of Provident fund till 6 April 2017 may not be taxable"), cites s.574A(3) and EIM75550, and even flags that the full pre-2017 deduction for an employer PF "depends on length of foreign service" — the only competitor found that mentions the service tests at all. Its worked example: lump sum £100, less pre-2017 value £40, less "25% of lumpsum £15," taxable £45. 1to1 Accountants ("UK Tax Treatment of Indian Provident Fund Withdrawals," 5 February 2026, read in full) presents the same framework with the same £100/£40 example — but computes the 25% step as "25% of your total lump sum (£25)," taxable £35. Two ranking guides, same fact pattern, same statute, answers £10 apart on a £100 withdrawal.

The statutory Step 3 is neither guide's version, quoted verbatim:

"Step 3 — If the lump sum is paid under an overseas pension scheme, deduct so much of the lump sum left after Step 2 as would, if the scheme were a registered pension scheme, not be liable to income tax under this Part."

Both guides apply a "25% tax-free" style deduction unconditionally. The statute conditions the entire step on the scheme meeting the UK's statutory definition of an "overseas pension scheme" — and measures the deduction by what a UK registered scheme could have paid tax-free, not by a flat quarter of the withdrawal. Neither guide, nor CoreAdviz, tests whether Indian EPF passes that gate. It is not obvious that it does.

The unsettled question no ranking guide asks

Is Indian EPF an "overseas pension scheme" under UK law at all?

"Overseas pension scheme" is a defined term (Finance Act 2004 s.150(7) plus SI 2006/206). HMRC's Pensions Tax Manual PTM112200 sets out the conditions. Two of them bite hard on EPF's facts:

The Tax Recognition Test. PTM112200, quoted verbatim: "So tax relief, or an exemption from tax, either applies at the point money goes into the scheme as a contribution, or the point it leaves as a benefit payment. It cannot be exempt at both of these points. Either the contribution into the scheme or most of the benefit payments out of it (excepting serious ill-health benefits) must be taxable." Indian EPF is, for most members, exempt at both points: employee contributions attract Section 80C relief, and withdrawals after five years of continuous service are exempt under ss.10(11)/10(12) of the Indian Income-tax Act — the very exemption all three competitor guides cite when explaining why India doesn't tax the withdrawal. Recent Indian carve-outs (tax on interest attributable to employee contributions above ₹2.5 lakh/year, and on employer contributions above ₹7.5 lakh/year) tax only the margin, not "all or most of the benefits." If EPF fails this test, it is not an "overseas pension scheme," and Step 3 — the 25%-style deduction both calculation guides apply — is simply unavailable.

The Regulation Requirements Test. PTM112200 again, verbatim: "If there is a regulator of occupational pension schemes in the country or territory in which the scheme is established, the scheme must be regulated by that regulator. If the occupational pension scheme is not regulated then it cannot be an overseas pension scheme. It is not open to the scheme to meet the requirement in another way." Whether EPF — a mandatory statutory fund administered by EPFO under its own Act, outside PFRDA's remit — is "regulated by" a pension regulator in the sense this test intends is a genuinely open classification question, structurally identical to the "is EPF a foreign grantor trust" question the US-side pages in this family turn on.

This page does not resolve the question, and has found no HMRC ruling, tribunal decision, or practitioner analysis that does. That is the point: the most generous deduction in the calculation every ranking guide presents as automatic hangs on a statutory definition nobody found has tested against EPF's actual features.

4. What HMRC's own worked example quietly demonstrates

EIM75550's example ("Tom") concerns a lump sum from an overseas personal pension scheme and begins with a sentence that should unsettle anyone applying Step 3 by default, quoted verbatim: "This scheme does not meet the conditions to be an overseas pension scheme within the meaning of section 150(7) FA 2004." HMRC's own illustrative taxpayer gets the Step 2 pre-2017 deduction — because his rights were specifically lump-sum rights — and, implicitly, no Step 3. The manual also confirms the Step 2 value is frozen: the deduction covers the 5 April 2017 value of pre-2017 lump-sum rights and "the value of the growth is not included in the step 2 deduction." A UK resident who lets an EPF balance ride for years before withdrawing is accruing fully-taxable value the whole time, whatever the pre-2017 balance was.

Adjacent development, easy to conflate

The July 2026 UK-India social security agreement does not touch any of this

On 15 July 2026 a National Insurance Double Contributions Convention between the UK and India came into force (gov.uk guidance published 7 July 2026, read directly). It determines which country's social security contributions a cross-border worker pays — National Insurance versus Indian schemes — for workers moving between the two countries. It is not an income tax treaty provision and does not change the UK income tax treatment of an EPF lump sum described above. Expect popular coverage to blur this line; the two regimes share the word "provident/social security" and nothing else relevant here.

Separately: this page is about EPF. For PPF, both better competitors correctly note (citing PTM021000 and SAIM2440) that a PPF account arguably fails the UK definition of a pension scheme entirely and gets taxed like a foreign deposit account, interest arising annually. That treatment was not independently re-derived here and is noted, not endorsed.

5. The India-UK DTAA: verified 2026-09-03, and it does not rescue anything above

This page and its sibling (Part 15) both previously flagged the India-UK Double Taxation Avoidance Convention's pension provisions as unread — a real gap, since a treaty allocating taxing rights to India exclusively would have overridden the entire ITEPA s.574A analysis above, the way Article 18 of the India-Canada treaty does for this family's Canada page (Part 16). It has now been read in full, directly at legislation.gov.uk (the 1993 Convention, SI 1993/1801, Schedule), cross-checked against secondary confirmation that the 2013 Protocol (SI 2013/3147) amended only Article 13 (royalties/fees for technical services) and added Article 28B (assistance in collection) — it did not touch the pensions, other-income, or relief articles read below, so the 1993 text quoted here is current.

Verified

Article 20 (Pensions and annuities) is a residence-state-exclusive rule — the opposite of the Canada treaty's Article 18

Article 20(1), quoted verbatim: "Any pension, other than a pension referred to in Article 19(2) of this Convention, or annuity paid to a resident of a Contracting State shall be taxable only in that State." For a UK-resident recipient, that is the UK — the same jurisdiction ITEPA s.574A already assumes taxes the lump sum. The treaty confirms the UK's right to tax; it supplies no relief India could claim instead, and none for the reader to claim against the UK computation above.

Article 20(2) then defines the term narrowly, quoted verbatim: "The term ‘pension’ means a periodic payment made in consideration of past employment or by way of compensation for injuries received in the course of performance of employment or any payments made under the social security legislation of either Contracting State." A one-off EPF lump-sum withdrawal is not a "periodic payment," so it plausibly falls outside Article 20's own definition of "pension" — structurally the same periodic-vs-lump-sum question this family hit in Part 8 (US treaty Article 20(3)) and Part 16 (the India-Canada treaty, which defines no "pension" term at all). Article 20(3)'s "annuity" definition, also quoted verbatim, requires "a stated sum payable periodically at stated times… under an obligation to make the payments in return for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth" — an EPF lump sum is the return of one's own contributions and interest, not a sum purchased for consideration, so this definition does not fit either.

Unlike Part 16's finding for Canada, this does not leave the lump sum in limbo. Article 23 ("Other income"), the treaty's residual sweep provision, quoted verbatim: "Subject to the provisions of paragraph (2) of this Article, items of income beneficially owned by a resident of a Contracting State, wherever arising… which are not dealt with in the foregoing Articles of this Convention, shall be taxable only in that State." Whether an EPF lump sum is classified as a treaty "pension" under Article 20 or falls through to Article 23 as "other income," both provisions use the identical residence-state-exclusive formula — "taxable only in that State" — and for a UK-resident recipient that State is the UK either way. The classification question that mattered decisively for Part 16 (because India-Canada Article 18 is source-state-exclusive, the opposite allocation) is here inert: it does not change which country gets the taxing right.

Article 3 (General definitions), read in full, defines no term for "pension" beyond what Article 20(2) itself supplies — there is no separate treaty-wide definition to reconcile.

Verified

Article 24 (Elimination of double taxation): the credit mechanism that would matter if India taxed anyway

Article 24(1)(a), quoted verbatim: "Indian tax payable under the laws of India and in accordance with the provisions of this Convention… on profits, income or chargeable gains from sources within India… shall be allowed as a credit against any United Kingdom tax computed by reference to the same profits, income or chargeable gains by reference to which the Indian tax is computed." The credit is only available for Indian tax "payable… in accordance with the provisions of this Convention" — since Article 20/23 allocate the taxing right to the UK exclusively, India has no treaty-conforming basis to tax the lump sum in the first place (and, as this page's own s.10(11)/10(12) reading shows, generally does not tax it as a domestic matter either after five years' continuous service). The credit mechanism is not what does the work here; the exclusive-allocation rule in Article 20/23 is.

HMRC's own Double Taxation Relief Manual entry for India, DT9553, read in full, covers only Articles 21 (students/trainees/researchers), 22 (teachers) and 24(4) (tax-sparing under s.80-IB/ 80-IC of the Indian Income-tax Act) — it contains no note on Article 19, 20 or 23 at all. That absence is itself a data point: HMRC's published India-specific digest has nothing to say about pension or lump-sum classification under this treaty, consistent with this page's finding elsewhere that no HMRC guidance, tribunal decision, or professional analysis addresses EPF's treaty classification specifically.

Questions worth putting to a UK-qualified adviser before withdrawing

  1. On what basis, if any, does my EPF meet the "overseas pension scheme" definition in s.150(7) FA 2004 and SI 2006/206 — given Indian tax exempts it at both contribution and withdrawal — and if it does not, what happens to the Step 3 deduction in your computation?
  2. Was my EPF an employer-financed retirement benefits scheme immediately before 6 April 2017? If yes, which of the s.574A(6)(f) foreign-service tests do I meet on my actual service history; if no, what evidence values my rights "specifically to receive benefits by way of lump sum payments" as at 5 April 2017?
  3. How much of my current balance is post-5-April-2017 interest and contributions — the part no version of the pre-2017 deduction reaches — and does withdrawing before establishing UK tax residence (or in a split year) change the analysis?
