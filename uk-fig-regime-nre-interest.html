The 4-Year FIG Regime Has a Hidden Cost — And a Free Alternative Sitting Right Next to It in the Treaty

This is the third UK-vertical page. The reader is an Indian-origin person newly UK tax resident since 6 April 2025, when the remittance basis was abolished and replaced by the Foreign Income and Gains (FIG) regime — a 4-year window in which qualifying foreign income and gains can be claimed free of UK tax. Every ranking guide checked here explains that NRE interest becomes UK-taxable once the FIG window closes, and that the India-UK tax treaty has a rare tax-sparing credit that can help then. None of them reads the FIG regime's own claim mechanics closely enough to notice that the treaty route is also available — for free — during the window, as an alternative to spending a FIG claim on NRE interest at all.

Roughly 1.86 million people of Indian origin live in England and Wales (2021 Census), and a steady stream of Skilled Worker, Global Talent and student-turned-professional arrivals lands every year with an NRE fixed deposit or savings account still earning interest back home. The old remittance basis let anyone shelter that interest indefinitely just by not bringing it to the UK. Finance Act 2025 ended that on 6 April 2025. What replaced it is narrower, time-limited, and — the point this page makes — not the only lever in the room.

1. What changed on 6 April 2025

The FIG regime is not an automatic exemption; it is an elective claim, made source by source, on a Self Assessment return. ITTOIA 2005 s.845A, inserted by Finance Act 2025 s.37(1)(4) with effect from the 2025-26 tax year, quoted verbatim from legislation.gov.uk:

"(1) An individual may make a claim for relief for a tax year under this section (a ‘foreign income claim’) if the individual is a qualifying new resident for that year (see section 845B). (2) Where an individual makes a foreign income claim for a tax year, the individual is entitled to relief for the tax year that is equal to so much of the total income of the individual for that year as — (a) reflects qualifying foreign income…, and (b) is identified as such in the claim. (3) The relief is given by deducting the amount of the relief in calculating the individual's net income for the tax year… (4) A foreign income claim must be made in a return. (5) A foreign income claim in relation to a tax year must be made before the end of the period of 12 months beginning with 31 January after the end of that tax year."

Two words in subsection (2)(b) — "identified as such" — do a lot of work this page comes back to: relief only reaches income the claim actually names. Nothing in s.845A requires an all-or-nothing claim covering every foreign income source a person has.

The 4-year window itself comes from s.845B, the "qualifying new resident" test, quoted verbatim:

"(1) For the purposes of this Chapter, an individual is a qualifying new resident for a tax year if — (a) the individual is UK resident for that tax year, (b) the individual is not disqualified for that tax year…, (c) for each of the 10 tax years before that tax year, the individual was not UK resident…, and (d) the individual is at least 10 years old at the commencement of that tax year. (2) An individual is also a qualifying new resident for a tax year if — (a) the individual is UK resident for that tax year, (b) the individual is not disqualified for that tax year, and (c) that tax year is one of the next three tax years after a qualifying tax year in relation to the individual."

Year of arrival (subsection (1)) plus "the next three tax years" (subsection (2)) is the 4-year window. The 10-consecutive-years-non-UK-resident test is the same single rule for a first-time arrival and for someone who lived in the UK years ago and has now been away for over a decade — there is no separate, harder test for "returners" hiding in the statute for a ranking guide to blur. (This closes one of three angles checked for this page, honestly: it is not the finding here.)

2. The cost every FIG claim carries, whatever it shelters

A claim under s.845A is not free. ITTOIA 2005 s.845E, inserted by the same Finance Act 2025 provision, quoted verbatim:

"Effect of claim, foreign employment election or foreign gain claim on personal allowance etc. Where an individual makes a foreign income claim, a foreign employment election or a foreign gain claim for a tax year, the individual is not entitled, for that year, to — (a) any allowance under Chapter 2 of Part 3 of ITA 2007 (personal allowance and blind person's allowance), (b) any tax reduction under Chapter 3 of that Part (tax reductions for married couples and civil partners), (c) any tax reduction under Chapter 3A of that Part (transferable tax allowance for married couples and civil partners), or (d) any relief under section 457 or 458 of ITA 2007 (payments for life insurance etc)."

The equivalent capital-gains cost sits in TCGA 1992 s.1K(6)(b), quoted verbatim: a foreign gain claim means "the individual has no entitlement to an annual exempt amount for that year." Both losses are whole-year and binary — claim relief on £50 of foreign income and the personal allowance (£12,570 for 2025-26) is gone for the year regardless of how small the claimed amount was, exactly as much as if £50,000 had been claimed. Because s.845A(2)(b) lets a claim name some foreign sources and not others, the live question for a reader with several types of foreign income is not "do I claim FIG" but "which sources go in the claim" — and NRE interest is the one source in this fact pattern that has a second, free way to reach the same result.

3. The treaty's tax-sparing credit is not a year-5 fallback — it is available now

The India-UK Double Taxation Avoidance Convention (SI 1993/1801, Schedule, read in full at legislation.gov.uk) taxes NRE interest under Article 12 (Interest), not under any special savings clause. Article 12(1)–(2), quoted verbatim: "Interest arising in a Contracting State and paid to a resident of the other Contracting State may be taxed in that other State. However, such interest may also be taxed in the Contracting State in which it arises…" — a normal concurrent-taxing-rights clause, capped at up to 15% source-state tax, with no NRE-specific carve-out anywhere in Article 12 itself.

The relief comes from Article 24 (Elimination of double taxation), paragraphs (3) and (4), quoted verbatim:

"(3) Subject to paragraph (5) of this Article, for the purposes of paragraph (1) of this Article the term ‘Indian tax payable’ shall be deemed to include: (a) any amount which would have been payable as Indian tax but for a deduction allowed in computing the taxable income or an exemption or reduction of tax granted for that year in question under the provisions of the Income-tax Act 1961… referred to in paragraph (4)(a)… (4) The provisions referred to in this paragraph are: (a) sections 10(4), 10(4B), 10(6)(viia), 10(15)(iv), 33AB, 80HHD, 80I and 80IA… (5) Relief from United Kingdom tax shall not be given by virtue of this paragraph (3)… if the income relates to a period starting more than 10 fiscal years after the… exemption from… Indian tax is first granted… in respect of that source."

Section 10(4) — NRE interest's home exemption — is named directly in Article 24(4)(a), not reached through the "agreed to be substantially similar" route Article 24(4)(b) provides for later provisions. India's own statute, s.10(4)(ii) of the Income-tax Act 1961, read in full at incometaxindia.gov.in, quoted verbatim: "in the case of an individual, any income by way of interest on moneys standing to his credit in a Non-Resident (External) Account in any bank in India in accordance with the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999…: Provided that such individual is a person resident outside India as defined in clause (w) of section 2 of the said Act or is a person who has been permitted by the Reserve Bank of India to maintain the aforesaid Account." The mechanism: India taxes NRE interest at 0% under s.10(4)(ii); Article 24(3)–(4) lets the UK pretend India collected tax anyway and give a credit for the amount "spared," which — because the UK's own tax on that interest is computed against the same amount — brings the UK bill to nil too, without a claim under s.845A, without touching s.845E, and without losing a penny of personal allowance.

Article 24(5)'s own 10-fiscal-year cap "per source" is the credit's real limit, not the FIG regime's 4 years — a materially longer runway for a reader weighing which mechanism to use on which income.

HMRC's own India digest

DT9553 confirms the mechanism exists, but never mentions NRE interest by name

HMRC's Double Taxation Relief Manual entry for India, DT9553, read in full, quoted verbatim: "Tax spared (Article 24(4)). The convention provides for credit to be given for tax ‘spared’… in India under the provisions of Indian law listed in Article 24(4)… Section 80-IB… has been agreed to be of a substantially similar character… Section 80-IC… is not agreed as being substantially similar… and no credit will be due… Relief is restricted to tax spared in India for a period of ten years in respect of any one source of income." DT9553 discusses only the s.80-IB/80-IC business- incentive provisions covered by Article 24(4)(b)'s "substantially similar" extension; it says nothing about s.10(4), which does not need that extension because Article 24(4)(a) names it outright. That silence is an honest gap in HMRC's published digest, not evidence against the credit — the treaty text itself is what does the work, and it names s.10(4) in the same paragraph as the business-incentive provisions DT9553 does discuss.

4. Where the two ranking guides checked here stop short

wealthnorth.in — the strongest UK-NRI guide found in earlier research for this project, updated June 2026, read in full — correctly names the mechanism, quoted verbatim:

"On NRE interest, the UK trap comes with a twist: the Indian exemption under Section 10(4)(ii) is Indian law only, so once outside the FIG window you owe UK tax on it as it arises — but this treaty contains a tax-sparing provision under which the UK may give a notional credit for the Indian tax forgone, subject to conditions and time limits. Worth raising with a UK adviser by name; few treaties offer it."

BKL, a UK accounting firm ("Indian bank interest: the UK tax consequences," 18 January 2026, read in full), is more technical and correctly cites the 10-year limit, quoted verbatim:

"Under this reduced FIG timeframe, it is likely that more NRIs will be liable for UK tax on their NRE and FCNR accounts… However, a special provision exists within the UK-India DTA known as Tax Sparing Relief. This allows UK resident investors to claim a notional tax credit… The relief applies for a 10-year period after the exemption… is first granted."

Both guides are accurate as far as they go, and both are better than most of what this project's earlier UK-vertical research found on adjacent topics. But both frame tax-sparing exclusively as what happens after the FIG window ends — wealthnorth's own words are "once outside the FIG window you owe UK tax on it… but this treaty contains a tax-sparing provision," and BKL's framing is that NRIs "will be liable for UK tax" under the "reduced FIG timeframe," with the tax-sparing relief presented as the remedy for that liability, not as an alternative that avoids triggering s.845A in the first place. Neither guide cites s.845A(2)(b)'s source-by-source claim wording, neither cites s.845E or TCGA s.1K's allowance-forfeiture rule, and neither states the order-of-operations point this page makes: a FIG-eligible reader with NRE interest and other foreign income (say, UK-source freelance income taxed abroad, or foreign dividends) can leave NRE interest out of the FIG claim, rely on Article 24 instead, and keep the personal allowance and CGT annual exempt amount intact for whatever else the claim does cover. Nothing found in either guide, or in the three competitor guides Part 14 read on the adjacent 2017-transition question, makes this connection.

What this page does not resolve

Whether HMRC will actually process the credit as easily as the statute suggests

Article 24(3)–(5) is a treaty entitlement, not a self-executing refund. Claiming it in practice means a foreign tax credit relief computation on the Self Assessment return, supported by evidence of the Indian exemption, and HMRC's own India-specific manual (DT9553) does not walk through the NRE-interest case at all — only the business-incentive one. Whether HMRC's caseworkers process an Article 24(4)(a)/s.10(4) tax-sparing claim on NRE interest routinely, or whether it draws the kind of scrutiny an unusual claim invites, was not established by any source read for this page. Nor was whether split-year treatment in the arrival year, or a change in FEMA residential status under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (which can require an NRE account to be re-designated once someone becomes "resident in India" again, cutting off the s.10(4)(ii) proviso's own condition), complicates the analysis for a specific reader's facts.

5. What is verified and what is not

  • VERIFIED — ITTOIA 2005 ss.845A, 845B and 845E, as inserted by Finance Act 2025 s.37(1)(4) (effective 2025-26 tax year onward), read in full at legislation.gov.uk: the FIG claim is elective, made in a return, source-by-source ("identified as such in the claim"), and carries a whole-year loss of the personal allowance, married-couple/civil-partner tax reductions, and life-insurance relief regardless of the size of the amount claimed.
  • VERIFIED — TCGA 1992 s.1K(6)(b), read in full: a foreign gain claim carries the same whole-year loss of the CGT annual exempt amount.
  • VERIFIED — ITTOIA 2005 s.845B(1)–(2), read in full: the 10-consecutive-years test and the 4-year window (arrival year plus three) apply identically to a first-time arrival and a returner with no prior-residence exception; the eligibility-blur angle originally flagged for this page does not survive contact with the statute.
  • VERIFIED — India-UK DTAA (SI 1993/1801), Articles 12 and 24, read in full at legislation.gov.uk: Article 12 has no NRE-specific exemption; Article 24(3)–(4) names Income-tax Act s.10(4) directly (not via the "substantially similar" extension) as a spared provision, subject to Article 24(5)'s 10-fiscal-year-per-source cap.
  • VERIFIED — Indian Income-tax Act 1961 s.10(4)(ii), read in full at incometaxindia.gov.in (cross-checked at indiankanoon.org): exempts NRE account interest for a person "resident outside India" under FEMA 1999, conditional on that residential status.
  • VERIFIED — HMRC DT9553, read in full: confirms the Article 24(4) tax-sparing mechanism exists and covers s.80-IB (not s.80-IC), but is silent on s.10(4)/NRE interest specifically — an honest gap in HMRC's own published digest, not a contrary finding.
  • VERIFIED — wealthnorth.in (updated June 2026) and BKL (18 January 2026), each read in full: both correctly identify the tax-sparing mechanism and its existence, and both frame it exclusively as a post-FIG-window remedy rather than a concurrent, source-specific alternative to a FIG claim.
  • INFERENCE — that HMRC will process an Article 24(4)/s.10(4) tax-sparing credit claim on NRE interest as a routine foreign tax credit relief computation. No HMRC manual guidance, tribunal decision, or practitioner case study walking through this specific claim was found; DT9553's silence on s.10(4) cuts both ways and was not resolved further.
  • Not resolved — the interaction with FEMA account re-designation and split-year UK residence treatment in the arrival year; both would need to be checked against a reader's actual dates before relying on anything above.

Questions worth putting to a UK-qualified adviser before filing a FIG claim

  1. Of my total foreign income and gains this year, which sources genuinely benefit from a FIG claim once the personal allowance, married-couple relief and CGT annual exempt amount are weighed against what each source would save — and does NRE interest need to be in that claim at all, given Article 24 is available as a separate route?
  2. Has Indian tax actually been "spared" on my NRE interest in a form Article 24(4)(a) recognises, and how many of the treaty's 10 fiscal years (per Article 24(5)) have already run against this source since I first held the account?
  3. Does my FEMA residential status support the s.10(4)(ii) proviso for the full tax year in question, and does a split UK tax year change which route is available for which part of the year?
