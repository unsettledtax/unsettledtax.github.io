Your EPF and gratuity payout isn’t a treaty “pension” — the Treasury’s own explanation says why

Every article that analyses an Indian EPF withdrawal or a Payment-of-Gratuity-Act lump sum received after moving to the US reaches for Article 20. The Treasury’s own Technical Explanation of the same treaty says, in nearly so many words, that a lump sum doesn’t qualify for Article 20 at all — and routes it somewhere with different consequences.

Sources read in full · US–India treaty TIAS 90-1218, Articles 1, 16, 20, 23 · Treasury Technical Explanation to the India treaty · Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972

Search the market’s own vocabulary — “EPF tax US resident,” “gratuity taxable US treaty” — and competent, dedicated content surfaces quickly. Golding & Golding’s EPF article is the strongest found: it quotes Article 20(1) of the India–US treaty verbatim and correctly separates it from Article 20(2)’s social-security rule. What it does not do, and what no competitor found does, is test whether the specific payment in front of the reader — almost always a single lump sum, not a stream of payments — is a treaty “pension” in the first place. The treaty defines the term, and defines it narrowly.

The one most often missed

1. The treaty’s own definition of “pension” excludes a lump sum

Article 20(3) of the US–India treaty does not leave “pension” undefined. It defines it once, for the whole article:

Article 20(3): “The term ‘pension’ means a periodic payment made in consideration of past services or by way of compensation for injuries received in the course of performance of services.”

The Treasury’s Technical Explanation to the treaty removes any doubt that “periodic” is doing real work here, and states directly what happens to a payment that fails the test:

Technical Explanation, Article 20: “...a payment must be, among other things, a periodic payment in order to qualify. Thus, all payments under this Article (pensions, annuities, alimony and child support) must be periodic payments, and a single lump-sum payment does not qualify. A pension under the U.S. Model is considered to include a single lump-sum payment. A single lump-sum payment received under a pension plan would be treated as other income under Article 23. Thus, such a payment would be taxable only in the Contracting State of which the income recipient is a resident unless the income arises in the other Contracting State. Income that arises in the other State may also be taxed by that other State.”

Two things follow. First, the US–India treaty is unusual: unlike the US Model treaty, which treats a lump sum as still within the pension article, this treaty’s own negotiated text carves lump sums out. Second, the Technical Explanation names exactly where a lump sum goes instead — Article 23, Other Income — and Article 23 works differently from Article 20(1) in a way that matters for the tax return, not just for classification.

2. Gratuity never qualifies, and most EPF withdrawals don’t either

Gratuity under India’s Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 is structurally incapable of passing the periodic-payment test. Every source describing it, without exception, describes it the same way: a single sum paid once, on termination, calculated from last-drawn salary and years of service — not a stream of payments. It is not a matter of how any one employee happens to receive it; the statute itself only provides for a one-time payment.

EPF is more mixed, and the mixing is exactly what the popular guidance collapses. The Employees’ Provident Fund and the Employees’ Pension Scheme are related but distinct arrangements under the same umbrella, with opposite payment shapes:

Employees’ Provident Fund (EPF)Employees’ Pension Scheme (EPS)
How it pays outLump-sum corpus withdrawal on leaving employmentMonthly pension from age 58 (early pension from 50), after 10+ years of pensionable service
Treaty Article 20(3) testFails — single payment, not periodicPasses — genuinely periodic
Governing article on these factsArticle 23 (Other Income)Article 20(1) (Private Pensions)

The EPF corpus — the part every guide on this topic is actually describing when it says “EPF withdrawal” — is the lump-sum piece. The genuinely periodic piece, EPS, is a separate scheme that most of the same articles do not distinguish from EPF at all.

3. What changes when the article is 23, not 20

Article 20(1) is an exclusive rule: a private pension is “taxable only” in the recipient’s state of residence, full stop, with no source-country carve-out written into the paragraph. Article 23 is not exclusive in the same way. Paragraph 1 gives the residence state the general right, matching Article 20(1)’s outcome for a US resident — but paragraph 3 adds something Article 20(1) does not have:

Article 23(3): “Notwithstanding the provisions of paragraphs 1 and 2, items of income of a resident of a Contracting state not dealt with in the foregoing articles of this Convention and arising in the other Contracting state may also be taxed in that other State.”

For a lump-sum EPF withdrawal or gratuity payment arising in India and received by a US resident, this means India keeps a real, treaty-sanctioned right to tax it too — not just a domestic-law claim the treaty happens not to block, but a right the treaty affirmatively preserves. That has a direct, practical consequence: whatever India withholds or assesses on the payment is a candidate for a US Foreign Tax Credit computation under IRC §901, in a way that a payment genuinely covered by Article 20(1)’s exclusive-residence rule would not cleanly be (there being, on that reading, no legitimate Indian tax to credit against in the first place). None of the three competitors checked for this page frames the question in FTC terms at all — all three ask only whether the US taxes the payment, not whether India is also entitled to, and whether that entitlement is what makes an FTC claim available and necessary rather than optional.

What the saving clause does and doesn’t change here

Article 23 is not on Article 1(4)(a)’s short list of provisions exempted from the saving clause (Article 9(2); Article 20 paragraphs 2 and 6; Articles 25, 26, 27 — the same list read in full for this family’s page 1 and page 4). That does not matter much on these facts: Article 23(1) already assigns the US, as residence state, taxing rights consistent with what the saving clause would produce anyway. What the saving clause does not reach or override is Article 23(3)’s preservation of India’s source-country right — the saving clause runs one direction, protecting a Contracting State’s ability to tax its own residents and citizens; it says nothing about the other state’s treaty-preserved right to tax income arising within it. That right stands on its own, independent of the saving clause entirely.

4. What the competitors say, checked directly against this

Three competitors were read against the market’s actual vocabulary (“Indian gratuity EPF lump sum received after becoming US tax resident treaty Article 16 sourcing” and variants):

  • Golding & Golding, “US Taxation of Indian Employee Provident Fund Plans (EPF)” (read in full) — the strongest of the three. It quotes Article 20(1) verbatim, correctly separates it from Article 20(2)’s social-security rule, and correctly explains what each means. It analyses every EPF distribution as an Article 20(1) pension without testing whether the specific payment is periodic, and does not mention Article 20(3)’s definition, the Technical Explanation, or Article 23 anywhere in the piece.
  • mytaxfiler.com, “EPF Tax Implications for Indian Professionals Who Moved to the US” (read in full) — states that EPF distributions are taxed as “gross distribution minus your cost basis” and that treaty relief “may exist in some circumstances” without naming Article 20, Article 23, any IRC section, or any Revenue Ruling anywhere in the article. This is the assertion-without-citation pattern the falsification step was looking for.
  • JustAnswer, “Gratuity Taxability for US Resident on Past India Service” — the search snippet states “Gratuity received by a US resident in 2018 for past services rendered while a nonresident in India is generally taxable in the US in the year of receipt.” The full answer sits behind JustAnswer’s paywall; this session could not reach it to check whether it cites a treaty article. Existence and the snippet claim are VERIFIED; the answer’s sourcing depth is not — flagged as inaccessible rather than assumed thin.

A separate query combining the treaty’s own “periodic payment” language with EPF and lump-sum terms returned no India-specific content connecting Article 20(3)’s definition, the Technical Explanation’s Article-23 redirect, and Indian EPF or gratuity at all. The one adjacent hit found — a 2017 JustAnswer thread quoting the same Article 20(3) language — concerns a US person’s own 401(k) and treaty exemption from Indian tax, the reverse fact pattern, and does not address EPF, gratuity, or the Article 23 redirect.

5. What this page leaves open

Not resolved here

Whether the cost basis in an EPF lump sum (employer contributions plus accumulated interest, against employee contributions that may already have been made from after-tax income) reduces the taxable amount, and under what specific IRC provision, was flagged by mytaxfiler.com but not independently verified against a primary source this pass — EPF is not a US-qualified plan, so there is no obvious §72 investment-in-the-contract mechanism to reach for by analogy, and this page does not assert one. Separately, whether EPF interest credited annually while the holder is already a US resident is currently includible each year (rather than only on eventual lump-sum withdrawal) is a distinct question this page does not address; page 1 of this series (PPF/EPF/NPS/ULIPs) covers a related but different question — the Rev. Proc. 2020-17 trust-classification angle — not this one. The Income-tax Act, 2025’s renumbering of the old Section 10(10) gratuity exemption and Section 10(12)/Rule 8 EPF exemption was checked only at the convergent-secondary level (multiple sources agree the exemptions moved into a dedicated Schedule effective 1 April 2026, but do not agree on a single section number), and is not relied on as a settled citation here since it does not bear on the treaty-classification finding this page is built on.

Questions worth putting to a preparer

  1. Is my EPF withdrawal a single lump sum, or am I actually receiving EPS monthly pension payments? These are different schemes with different treaty treatment.
  2. If it’s a lump sum, has the position been analysed under Article 20 or under Article 23 — and if Article 20, has the “periodic payment” definition in Article 20(3) been tested against the actual payment received?
  3. Did India withhold or assess any tax on the gratuity or EPF lump sum? If so, is that being claimed as a Foreign Tax Credit, and on what basis is that credit available given the governing treaty article?
  4. Is the position that Article 20 applies here consistent with the Treasury’s own Technical Explanation, which states plainly that a single lump-sum payment under this treaty does not qualify as a “pension”?
