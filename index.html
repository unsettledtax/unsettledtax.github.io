Four questions about Indian assets that US tax guidance answers confidently, and shouldn’t

PPF, EPF, NPS and ULIPs, read against the primary sources rather than the secondary commentary.

Sources read in full · Rev. Proc. 2020-17 · US–India treaty TIAS 90-1218 · Treasury Technical Explanation · Rev. Proc. 2003-78

There is no IRS Revenue Ruling, Private Letter Ruling, Chief Counsel Advice, Tax Court opinion or IRS publication that names PPF, EPF, NPS or ULIPs and states how they are taxed. Every confident answer you will read is an extrapolation from adjacent authority. That is not a criticism of the people writing them — it is the situation. What follows is what the primary documents actually say, and where they stop.

The one most often missed

1. Article 20 gives a US citizen nothing

The familiar argument is that Article 20 of the US–India treaty protects Indian pension income. The clause that removes it is two articles earlier.

Article 1(3), the saving clause: “Notwithstanding any provision of the Convention except paragraph 4, a Contracting State may tax its residents … and by reason of citizenship may tax its citizens, as if the Convention had not come into effect.”

Article 1(4)(a) then lists what survives that clause. For Article 20 it names paragraphs 2 and 6 only — social security and child support. Paragraph 1, the private-pension rule, is not on the list.

The Treasury Technical Explanation states it directly: paragraphs 2 and 6 “are not subject to the saving clause.” By plain negative implication, paragraph 1 is.

So for a US citizen, Article 20(1) offers no protection at all. A narrow argument survives only for someone outside the US tax net entirely — not a green-card holder, and not a citizen. If a preparer relies on Article 20, ask them to address the saving clause by name.

2. PPF probably fails Rev. Proc. 2020-17 — on a condition nobody discusses

Rev. Proc. 2020-17 can relieve a “tax-favored foreign retirement trust” of Forms 3520 and 3520-A. Almost every discussion of PPF checks the contribution ceiling, and PPF passes it comfortably — ₹1.5 lakh a year sits far below the $50,000 limit.

The ceiling is not the binding condition. Section 5.03(3) requires that contributions come from personal-services income. A PPF account can be funded by anyone: a parent funding a minor’s account, or contributions made out of investment income. Those accounts appear to fail regardless of how small the balance is.

Condition 5.03(2) is also unclear for PPF. It requires that information be reported, or otherwise available, to the local tax authority — and PPF has no Form 26AS equivalent. EPF fares better here, through UAN-linked EPFO records.

The prior question

All of that assumes PPF is a trust at all. That threshold question is itself unresolved: practitioners variously treat it as a foreign grantor trust, an ordinary foreign account, or a non-grantor trust. It decides whether Form 3520 applies before relief is even considered — and no authority chooses among them.

3. The insurance excise-tax exemption has a $75,000 gate

Article 2(1)(a) does make the §4371 excise tax on foreign insurance premiums a covered tax. Unusually, the India treaty delivers relief through Article 7 business-profits protection rather than a dedicated exemption clause of the kind found in the German and French treaties.

But treaty eligibility is not the operative test. Rev. Proc. 2003-78 §3.01 requires an affirmative closing agreement:

“… prior to filing the return for the taxable period, such person has knowledge that there was in effect for such taxable period a closing agreement between the Internal Revenue Service and the foreign insurer or reinsurer…”

That agreement requires a sworn statement of treaty residency, an EIN, named responsible parties, and a $75,000 irrevocable letter of credit in the IRS’s favour. No public evidence was found that any Indian insurer holds one, and Rev. Proc. 2003-78 is rarely mentioned in commentary on Indian policies at all.

4. NPS Tier I and Tier II are different questions

Most guidance answers “NPS” as a single thing. The two tiers diverge on exactly the conditions that matter.

Lock-inTo age 60, with mandatory annuitisation of at least 40% at exitNone — freely withdrawable

Rev. Proc. 2020-17 §5.03(1)Plausibly satisfiedAppears to fail

NPS carries a second, layered problem in either tier: its pension fund managers invest in pooled vehicles that may themselves be PFICs inside the trust wrapper. No ruling addresses whether look-through applies.

Questions worth putting to a preparer

  1. Which entity classification are you using for my PPF — grantor trust, non-grantor trust, or not a trust at all? That decides whether Form 3520 applies before anything else.
  2. Are you testing each Rev. Proc. 2020-17 condition individually, including 5.03(3) and 5.03(2), rather than only the contribution limit?
  3. Is my NPS Tier II being analysed separately from Tier I?
  4. Have you tested my ULIP or endowment policy against §7702, or assumed it is insurance because it is called insurance in India?
  5. For any §4371 position, does the insurer hold a Rev. Proc. 2003-78 closing agreement?
  6. Am I a US citizen, or a resident who is not a citizen? That decides whether Article 20(1) is available at all.
  7. Is your position supported by an IRS ruling, or is it your reasoned application of adjacent authority to a fact pattern the IRS has never addressed?

This is not tax advice. It is a reading of primary documents, published so that the questions can be asked. It creates no professional relationship, computes no tax, and should not be relied on for a filing position. Where the sources are silent, this page says so rather than filling the gap.
