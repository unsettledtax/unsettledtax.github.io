Two High Courts Disagree About Your American Employee's EPF. The New 2026 Scheme Picked a Side Nobody Has Noticed.

A US citizen working for an Indian company is legally an "International Worker" under EPF law. Whether that means mandatory provident fund contributions on their entire US-scale salary — with no ₹15,000 ceiling and no withdrawal before age 58 — has been struck down as unconstitutional by one High Court, upheld by another, and is now sitting, unresolved, in front of the Supreme Court. Then, on 29 June 2026, the entire scheme both courts were arguing about was replaced. No source found connects any of this to what it means for FBAR, FATCA, or the size of the account balance a US person has to report.

This family's own Part 12 read para 43-A of the Employees' Pension Scheme, 1995 at EPFO's own site and called it "the live current scheme text." That framing needs a correction, and the correction is a story in itself: para 43-A, and its EPF-side twin para 83, are not settled text at all. They are the subject of a live three-court fight over whether treating "International Workers" — including every US citizen or green-card holder on an Indian payroll — differently from ordinary Indian employees is constitutional, and the scheme that housed both provisions no longer legally exists in the form either court was examining.

What para 83 and para 43A actually did, read from the provisions themselves

1. Full salary, no ceiling, no early exit — unless your country has a deal with India

Introduced by notification dated 1 October 2008, para 83 of the EPF Scheme, 1952 pulled every "International Worker" — defined broadly enough to include a foreign passport-holder employed in India — out of the ordinary ₹15,000/month wage ceiling that caps mandatory PF contributions for domestic employees. An IW's employer and employee contributions are calculated on full salary, however large. Para 43A did the pension-side equivalent, and layered on a second restriction: an IW cannot withdraw the accumulated corpus before age 58 unless totalisation applies under a Social Security Agreement (SSA) between India and the worker's country — the same para 14(1) mechanism this family's Part 12 already examined. India has SSAs with 20 countries. The United States is not one of them.

2. Karnataka struck it down. The reasoning is aimed squarely at non-SSA-country workers.

On 25 April 2024, the Karnataka High Court (Justice K.S. Hemalekha), deciding a batch of writ petitions going back to 2010, struck down both provisions. The operative order, read in full and quoted verbatim:

"ORDER (i) Writ Petitions are allowed. (ii) The introduction of para 83 of Employees' Provident Fund Scheme and para 43A of Employees' Pension Scheme are hereby struck down as unconstitutional and arbitrary and consequently, all the orders passed thereof are unenforceable."

The reasoning that got there is the part no competitor content connects to a US person's fact pattern. The Union of India argued para 83 exists to honour reciprocal SSA obligations. The Court rejected that justification specifically because it does not fit workers from non-SSA countries, quoted verbatim:

"An international worker from a non-SSA country is not allowed to withdraw accumulation until he reaches the age of 58 years. Therefore, para 83 eventually applies to international workers from countries with which the Government of India does not have SSA, and therefore, the claim of reciprocity does not arise and thus the claim of the Government that the obligation of reciprocity has made the Government of India to enact para 83 is unsustainable."

In other words: the Court's own logic for striking the provision down turns on exactly the group this family's audience sits in — American employees of Indian companies, for whom no SSA reciprocity argument has ever applied. It is also brought out, and quoted in the judgment, that "only twenty countries have entered into SSA agreement with India although para 83 was introduced long before." The judgment goes further and reasons the classification fails ordinary Article 14 scrutiny on its own terms — unlimited PF liability on rich expatriates crowding out an enforcement apparatus built for low-wage domestic workers — but the non-SSA-country holding is the one this page is built on, because it is the one that applies to a US citizen specifically, independent of income level.

The split, read from the second judgment directly

3. Delhi disagreed — expressly, and eighteen months later

On 4 November 2025, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court (SpiceJet Ltd vs Union of India, W.P.(C) 2941/2012 and connected petitions) reached the opposite conclusion and said so directly, quoted verbatim:

"Learned counsel for the petitioner has placed reliance on the judgment of the Karnataka High Court in Stone Hill Education Foundation (supra)... We, however, are unable to agree with the reasoning given and conclusions arrived at by the learned Single Judge of the Karnataka High Court in the said judgment..."

The Delhi Bench upheld the 2008 and 2010 notifications introducing para 83 in full, dismissing every connected petition. Two further facts complete the picture, both independently VERIFIED: first, EPFO's own appeal against the Karnataka ruling (W.A. No.887/2024) was still pending before a Karnataka Division Bench as of an April 2025 single-judge order in a separate case (Intel Technology India) that applied the Karnataka precedent anyway and noted "pendency of the appeal cannot be a ground" to withhold relief. Second, the Supreme Court issued notice on a plea challenging para 83 on 12 March 2026 and has not yet ruled. As of this page's publication, the constitutional question a US-citizen IW's employer would ask — does full-salary, no-ceiling, no-early-exit PF apply to this employee — has three unresolved proceedings running in parallel, in three different courts, with no binding national answer.

4. Then, on 29 June 2026, the scheme both courts were arguing about was replaced

Gazette notification G.S.R. 525(E), dated 29 June 2026, notified the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme, 2026 under the Code on Social Security, 2020, effective 1 July 2026 — superseding the EPF Scheme, 1952 (and, in parallel, the EPS Scheme, 2026 superseding the 1995 Pension Scheme) wholesale. This is independently corroborated by a PIB press release, the Ministry of Labour's own notification, and convergent professional coverage (KPMG, Nexdigm, Lawrbit, Lexology, SCC Online), all naming the same G.S.R. number and date.

WTW's own July 2026 client note on the new scheme — read in full, not title-matched — states the International Worker treatment plainly, and it is a real change, not a renumbering exercise:

"As per the current reading of EPF 2026, the statutory ceiling (INR 15,000 per month) for the consideration of 'Wage' for IWs, seems to apply. However, an employee, employed in another country specified under Paragraph 9(6), shall have the option to be detached from the other country's social security plan. In such case, he shall contribute on wage without application of the statutory ceiling."

Read plainly: EPF 2026 appears to have quietly adopted the Karnataka position as the default rule — the ₹15,000 ceiling now applies to International Workers generally — while carving out a narrower uncapped exception tied specifically to SSA-country detached status under the new scheme's own Paragraph 9(6). A US citizen employed in India is not covered by any SSA and cannot be "detached" from a US social security plan under an agreement that does not exist, so on WTW's own reading, a US-citizen IW's mandatory contributions should now fall under the capped default, not the old uncapped para-83 treatment. WTW hedges this twice in three sentences ("current reading," "seems to apply") — a signal that even a top-tier professional firm is inferring this from the new text rather than citing an EPFO circular that states it outright. This page adopts the same caution: this is the most direct reading of a primary professional source, not an EPFO-confirmed position, and it does not resolve what happens to contributions already made under the old uncapped rule between 2008 and 30 June 2026.

Update, 3 September 2026

WTW's reading above has been superseded by a corrigendum — the ₹15,000 ceiling does not apply to International Workers by default

On 4 August 2026, the Ministry of Labour and Employment issued G.S.R. 703(E), a corrigendum to the EPF Scheme, 2026 (Gazette of India, Extraordinary, Part II, Section 3(i), No. 639, dated 4 August 2026), correcting 25 drafting errors across the original G.S.R. 525(E) text. Two of those corrections sit directly inside the membership paragraph WTW's reading turned on:

"in page 66… line 32, for ‘employee’, read ‘employee other than the International Worker’."
"in page 76… line 1, for ‘member’, read ‘member other than international workers’."

Read together with contemporaneous reporting (Business Standard, 16–17 July 2026, and corroborating notes from CMS INDUSLAW and Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas & Co.), EPFO's own position — stated before the corrigendum was even gazetted — was that the ambiguous wording was a drafting inconsistency, not a policy change: "the provisions relating to international workers had been carried forward 'substantially in the same manner' from the Employees' Provident Fund Scheme, 1952." The corrigendum's own text now excludes International Workers from the general-member wage-ceiling paragraph by name. Put plainly: WTW's July reading (₹15,000 ceiling applies to IWs by default under EPF Scheme 2026, Paragraph 9(6) detachment aside) was based on the pre-corrigendum text and has not held up. The pre-2026 position — full salary, no ceiling, for International Workers from non-SSA countries including the United States — appears to continue unchanged into EPF Scheme 2026, subject to the same Paragraph 9(6)/SSA-detachment carve-out already described above. This is still INFERENCE, now two levels removed from EPFO's notified text (secondary reporting of EPFO's stated position, plus this page's own reading of the corrigendum's verbatim wording, sourced from a Gazette-text reproduction on a professional-compliance site, not the corrigendum PDF itself read directly) — a preparer should still confirm against the corrigendum PDF or a written EPFO circular before relying on it. It does not touch the Supreme Court question in Section 3 above (no hearing or order beyond the 12 March 2026 notice was found as of this update) or the age-58 withdrawal lock (Section 4, next paragraph).

On withdrawal timing, WTW's note confirms the age-58 lock survives into the new scheme unchanged for non-SSA IWs: "With the exception of IW cases covered by SSA and equivalent arrangements, withdrawals would be permissible after attaining 58 years of age" — meaning even if the 2026 scheme shrinks a US person's ongoing contribution base, it does not touch the separate, harder-edged restriction this family's Part 12 already covers: money already in the account stays locked until 58 regardless.

5. EPFO's own public-facing pages have not caught up — checked directly, today

Two months after the replacement took legal effect, EPFO's own live scheme pages at pmvbry.epfindia.gov.in — the same domain this family's Part 8 and Part 12 cited as authoritative primary text — still describe the superseded schemes as current. The EPF-scheme summary page, checked directly on 30 August 2026, opens: "The Employees' Provident Funds Scheme, 1952, is a social security initiative in India that is administered by the Employees' Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO)..." with no mention of the 2026 replacement anywhere on the page. The companion EPS-scheme page still states "This Scheme shall come into force on 16th day of November, 1995" as its operative commencement clause. Neither page contains the string "2026," "Paragraph 9(6)," or any reference to the Karnataka/Delhi split. This is not a criticism of EPFO so much as a concrete illustration of this project's own standing rule: a government source publishing text is not the same as that text being current, and a domain that rewarded direct reading twice before (Parts 8 and 12) needs to be re-checked, not assumed still accurate, once a wholesale legislative replacement is independently confirmed elsewhere.

6. What no competitor connects to a US person's own reporting obligations

The closest dedicated competitor found — Kewal Krishan & Co's "US Citizens in India: EPF Reporting & Form 2555 Guide" (kkca.io, dated 6 August 2026, three weeks after WTW's note and read in full, not title-matched) — is a genuinely current, well-organized mapping of EPF components to Form 2555, Form 1040 Schedule B, Form 3520, and FinCEN Form 114. It states plainly that "the EPF is a government-managed financial account held outside the United States, it is a reportable asset. You must include its maximum annual balance on FinCEN Form 114 if your combined foreign accounts top $10,000." It never mentions paragraph 83, the wage ceiling, the Karnataka or Delhi rulings, or the EPF Scheme 2026's Paragraph 9(6) carve-out anywhere in the piece — it treats the account balance a US-citizen IW is required to report as a simple, uncontested fact rather than the product of an unsettled, currently-litigated classification question that could retroactively change the size of future contributions. A dedicated follow-up search combining "paragraph 83," "International Worker," and any of "US citizen," "FATCA," "FBAR," or "expat" returned no matching content connecting the litigation to US tax reporting in either direction.

7. What is confirmed and what is not

  • VERIFIED — the Karnataka High Court struck down para 83 of the EPF Scheme, 1952 and para 43A of the EPS Scheme, 1995 as unconstitutional and arbitrary on 25 April 2024, read from the full judgment text (Value and Budget Housing Corporation & Ors vs Union of India).
  • VERIFIED — the Delhi High Court expressly declined to follow that reasoning and upheld the same two notifications on 4 November 2025, read from the full judgment text (SpiceJet Ltd vs Union of India).
  • VERIFIED — EPFO's own appeal against the Karnataka ruling (W.A. No.887/2024) remained pending as of an April 2025 order in a separate case, and the Supreme Court issued notice on a challenge to para 83 on 12 March 2026; neither had produced a final, binding national ruling as of this page's publication.
  • VERIFIED — the EPF Scheme, 2026 and EPS Scheme, 2026 were notified by G.S.R. 525(E) dated 29 June 2026 under the Code on Social Security, 2020, effective 1 July 2026, superseding the schemes both courts examined.
  • SUPERSEDED — WTW's July 2026 reading that EPF Scheme 2026's Paragraph 9(6) mechanism defaults International Workers to the ordinary ₹15,000 wage ceiling was based on text later corrected. See the 3 September 2026 update box above.
  • VERIFIED — the Ministry of Labour and Employment issued G.S.R. 703(E), a corrigendum to the EPF Scheme 2026, on 4 August 2026, correcting the membership paragraph to read "member other than international workers," read from the Gazette text itself.
  • INFERENCE, now sourced from the corrigendum's own corrected wording plus secondary reporting of EPFO's stated position (Business Standard, CMS INDUSLAW, Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas & Co.), not a dedicated EPFO circular or FAQ — that EPF Scheme 2026, as corrected, preserves the old uncapped, full-salary treatment for International Workers from non-SSA countries, including the United States, rather than capping them at ₹15,000. A practitioner should confirm this reading against the corrigendum PDF itself or a written EPFO circular before relying on it.
  • Not resolved — what happens, under the 2026 scheme's continuity-of- membership provisions, to contributions a US-citizen IW already made on an uncapped basis between 2008 and 30 June 2026, including whether that pre-2026 corpus is treated any differently on eventual withdrawal than a post-2026 capped contribution stream. This page does not attempt to answer that; no source found addresses it.

What this means for Part 12

This family's own Part 12 read para 43-A's totalisation-and-withdrawal-benefit test as "the live current scheme text" without flagging that the provision's constitutional validity was, at the time of writing, already the subject of a Karnataka/Delhi split and a pending Supreme Court notice, and that it has since been superseded outright by the schemes described above. Part 12's reading of para 14(1)'s "date of exit" anchor is not contradicted by anything found here — the totalisation-eligibility mechanism it describes is a separate question from the wage-ceiling and withdrawal-age fight this page covers — but the framing of EPFO's site as settled, current text was already imprecise when it was written, and is now clearly stale. This page functions as a live correction: read Part 12 for the totalisation mechanism, and read this page for the fact that the provision housing it has been litigated, replaced, and not yet re-confirmed by EPFO in the form Part 12 quoted.
