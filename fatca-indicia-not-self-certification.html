“Keep your FATCA self-certification updated” is not what the regulation says the bank has to do

Every guide on updating your Indian bank after a move to the US treats the self-certification form as the thing that decides your FATCA status. The India-US IGA, word for word, gives the bank’s own file — not your form — the power to overrule it.

Sources read in full · CBDT Guidance Note on Implementation of Reporting Requirements under Rules 114F–114H, 31 Dec 2015 (reproducing Annex I of the India-US FATCA IGA verbatim) · Income-tax Rules, 1962, Rules 114F–114H

Search the market’s own vocabulary — “FATCA self-certification not updated,” “Indian bank still thinks I’m a resident” — and two genuinely competent results surface: a detailed article from Golding & Golding, a US international-tax law firm, and a shorter piece from indiafornri.com. Both correctly explain that Indian banks report US-person accounts to the IRS under the India-US Inter-Governmental Agreement (IGA), and both advise keeping your self-certification current. Neither reads the due-diligence text of the IGA itself, reproduced word for word in CBDT’s own Guidance Note. That text does not describe self-certification as the control. It describes the bank’s own account record — address, phone number, standing instructions, power of attorney, place of birth — as the control, with self-certification demoted to one input the bank is affirmatively required to override when the record contradicts it.

The one most often missed

1. A “change in circumstances” forces the bank to disregard your own self-certification, not just refresh it

The advice repeated everywhere is to keep your FATCA/CRS self-certification form current. That is necessary. It is not the mechanism the regulation actually describes for what happens when it goes stale. Paragraph 5.7.6 of the Guidance Note — on new individual accounts, where a self-certification was validly obtained at account opening — states the rule directly:

Paragraph 5.7.6: “Where a self-certification has been obtained for a new individual account and if there is a change of circumstances with respect to such account which causes the reporting financial institution to know, or have reason to know, that the said self-certification is incorrect or unreliable, the reporting financial institution shall not rely on the said self-certification and shall obtain a valid self-certification… If the Reporting Financial Institution is unable to obtain a valid self-certification, the Reporting Financial Institution shall treat the account as a reportable account with respect to each such country or territory outside India for which indicia is identified.”

Read that sequence again: the bank does not wait for you to update anything. A change in circumstances that contradicts your form — a new address, a new phone number, standing instructions to move funds abroad — puts the bank under a duty to stop relying on what you told it, and to report the account as reportable by default if it cannot get a corrected form. The form is not the source of truth the bank defers to. It is a claim the bank’s own record can override.

2. The same logic runs through preexisting accounts, via “indicia,” not self-certification at all

For accounts that existed before FATCA due diligence began, there was often no self-certification to begin with. Annex I of the IGA — reproduced verbatim in the Guidance Note’s appendix — instead runs entirely on indicia: facts already sitting in the bank’s electronic and paper records.

Annex I, section II.B(1): the bank must search its records for “(a) Identification of the Account Holder as a U.S. citizen or resident; (b) Unambiguous indication of a U.S. place of birth; (c) Current U.S. mailing or residence address… (d) Current U.S. telephone number; (e) Standing instructions to transfer funds to an account maintained in the United States; (f) Currently effective power of attorney or signatory authority granted to a person with a U.S. address; or (g) An ‘in-care-of’ or ‘hold mail’ address that is the sole address… on file.”

Annex I, section II.B(3): “If any of the U.S. indicia … are discovered in the electronic search, or if there is a change in circumstances that results in one or more U.S. indicia being associated with the account, then the Reporting Indian Financial Institution must treat the account as a U.S. Reportable Account unless it elects to apply” the curing exceptions.

None of this asks what the account holder self-certified. It asks what is already in the file. An Indian resident’s account with no US address, no US phone number and no US place of birth on file generates no indicia at all and is not touched by this mechanism — which cuts the other way against the popular fear (repeated in general form by the Golding & Golding piece) that simply not updating a bank after a move to the US is, by itself, what triggers detection. It is not the silence that triggers it. It is a specific, listed fact landing in the bank’s own record.

3. “Curing” an indicium needs a specific document set, not an updated form

Where an indicium is found, the IGA does allow the bank to treat the account as non-reportable anyway — but only by collecting a defined document set, not by accepting an updated self-certification form on its own. For most indicia (a foreign mailing address, phone number, standing instruction, or power of attorney), paragraph 5.4.7 of the Guidance Note requires a self-certification plus independent documentary evidence supporting it. For the specific case of an unambiguous US place of birth, the bar is higher again:

Paragraph 5.4.8, curing a US place of birth: the bank must obtain (i) “a self-certification that the account holder is neither a citizen of the United States of America nor its resident for tax purposes”; (ii) “a passport or other Government-issued identification evidencing the account holder’s citizenship or nationality in a country other than the United States”; and (iii) “a copy of the account holder’s certificate of loss of nationality of the United States… or a reasonable explanation of — (1) the reason[s] the account holder does not have such a certificate despite relinquishing citizenship… or (2) the reason[s] the account holder did not obtain citizenship of the United States at birth.”

A person with a US place of birth on file — someone born during a parent’s posting, study, or work stint in the US and later raised and resident in India — sits inside this specific three-document curing requirement the moment that field is on record, regardless of whether they have ever held US citizenship, a green card, or US tax residence. Neither competitor found addresses the place-of-birth curing rule at all.

4. What each trigger actually requires

SituationGoverning textWhat the bank must do
Self-cert given, later contradicted by a change in circumstancesGuidance Note §5.7.6Stop relying on the old self-cert; get a new one or treat the account as reportable by default
Preexisting account, no US indicia foundAnnex I §II.B(2)No action required — until a change in circumstances introduces an indicium
Preexisting account, an indicium found (address, phone, standing instruction, POA)Annex I §II.B(3)-(4); Guidance Note §5.4.7Reportable by default, unless cured with self-cert + independent documentary evidence
Preexisting account, unambiguous US place of birthAnnex I §II.B(4)(a); Guidance Note §5.4.8Reportable by default, unless cured with self-cert + non-US passport + CLN or a stated reason for neither
High-value account with a relationship manager who has actual knowledgeAnnex I §III.B(4)Reportable regardless of what any form says

Neither quotes Annex I, the Guidance Note, or any curing provision; both frame the fix as “update your self-certification,” not as the regulation’s own change-in-circumstances trigger or its documentary curing requirements. Questions worth putting to a preparer or the bank

  1. Has the bank actually identified any of the seven listed US indicia on this account, or is the concern purely that the self-certification form is out of date?
  2. If an indicium exists, has it been cured with the specific document set the IGA requires (self-certification plus independent documentary evidence), or only with an updated form?
  3. If the indicium is an unambiguous US place of birth, has the three-part curing set (non-US passport, plus a certificate of loss of nationality or a stated reason for not having one) actually been assembled, not just discussed?
  4. Has anyone asked the bank whether a relationship manager has actual knowledge of US status that would make the indicia analysis moot?
  5. Separately from what the bank reports: has Form 8938 and the FBAR been filed to match what the bank is required to report, regardless of how the bank’s own determination comes out?
