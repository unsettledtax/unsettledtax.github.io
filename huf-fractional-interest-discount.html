The fractional interest discount was built for a fixed share in a US partition court. A coparcenary interest is neither.

American estate and gift tax law has a forty-year-old, well-litigated answer for what an undivided co-ownership share is worth: less than its proportionate slice of the whole, discounted for the cost, delay, and uncertainty of forcing a sale. Every case that built that answer assumed two things a Hindu Undivided Family coparcenary interest does not have — a fixed percentage, and a partition remedy under a US court’s own law. No source found addresses what happens to the discount analysis when both assumptions fail at once.

Sources read in full · 26 CFR 20.2031-1(b) · case-law lineage via a named practitioner summary quoting the opinions · Order XX Rule 18, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (convergent secondary, not bare-act-read) · builds on parts 6 and 10

Part 10 of this series left one question open: how is a coparcener’s undivided, unpartitioned share of HUF property valued for US estate tax purposes under the willing-buyer/willing-seller standard, given that Hindu law makes the interest severable only by partition and its size shifts with births and deaths in the family rather than by market transaction? This page does not close that question. It does something narrower: it identifies the exact body of US law that would normally answer a question that looks like this one — and shows why that body of law, as written and litigated, does not actually reach an HUF interest without someone doing work no source found has done.

The doctrine that exists

1. US estate and gift tax law already discounts undivided co-ownership interests — it just has never been tested against one governed by foreign personal law

The valuation standard is the same one this series already read in full for Part 10:

26 CFR 20.2031-1(b): “The fair market value is the price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or to sell and both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts.”

Applied to an undivided fractional interest in real property — a tenancy in common, the closest thing US law has to a coparcener’s position — that standard has produced a specific, decades-old doctrine called the fractional interest discount (also called the undivided interest discount). The IRS spent years arguing the only permissible discount was the bare cost of a partition action; the Tax Court and the Ninth and Fifth Circuits rejected that position repeatedly. A detailed case-law summary by Stephen R. Akers, senior fiduciary counsel at Bessemer Trust, read in full and quoting the opinions directly, traces the lineage:

Samuel J. LeFrak v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 1993-526 (quoted in the summary): the cost of partition is only one of three factors, and “uncertainty, and delays attendant upon partition proceedings” must also be considered — 20% minority discount plus 10% marketability discount allowed.

Estate of Ellie Williams, T.C. Memo 1998-59: the court rejected a bare cost-of-partition approach because it “does not address lack of control and marketability issues,” noting that “the absence of sales of fractional interests evidenced a lack of marketability” — a 44% combined discount allowed for an undivided timberland interest.

Estate of Baird, T.C. Memo. 2001-258, aff’d in relevant part, 416 F.3d 442 (5th Cir. 2005): a 60% discount allowed for a fractional timberland interest, later held to entitle the estate to litigation costs because the IRS’s cost-of-partition-only position was not substantially justified.

Ludwick v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2010-104: Judge Halpern built a weighted model — the present value of sale proceeds under a two-year contested partition, weighted against the present value of an uncontested one-year sale — landing on a 17.2% discount for a 50% undivided interest in a Hawaiian vacation home.

Across the cases this summary surveys (Propstra, 9th Cir. 1982; Cervin, 1994; Pillsbury, 1992; Feuchter, 1992; Baggett, 1991; Mooneyham, 1991; Anderson, 1988; Van Loben Sels, 1986; McCormick, 1995; and others), allowed discounts for undivided real-property interests cluster around 15–30%, with outliers from 0% (where the estate failed to prove a discount was warranted at all) up to 60%. This is a real, mature, heavily litigated doctrine, not a theoretical extension of the FMV standard. A dedicated search combining it with HUF or coparcenary terms (“fractional interest discount” “Hindu Undivided Family” OR HUF OR coparcenary estate tax) returned nothing connecting the two anywhere.

2. Two structural mismatches the doctrine has never had to solve at once

Every case above shares two features that a coparcenary interest does not:

a. A fixed, already-known percentage

LeFrak’s gifts were of interests under 10% each; Ludwick’s were exactly 50%; Baird’s were 14/65 and 17/65. In every case, the court’s job started from an undisputed fraction and asked only how much to discount it. A coparcener’s share is not that kind of number. As Part 6 of this series established from 26 CFR 301.7701-3’s liability test and corroborating Hindu-law sources, and as Part 10 restates, the coparcenary body fluctuates by birth and death — a coparcener’s fractional share is not fixed at the valuation date the way a 50% tenancy-in-common deed is. The fractional interest discount doctrine answers “how much less is X% worth than X% of the whole.” It has no branch for “what is X, and does it stay X between the valuation date and any eventual partition.”

b. A partition remedy under the same sovereign’s law the case was decided in

Ludwick’s court modeled the cost and delay of a partition action under Hawaii procedure; Baird’s modeled Louisiana partition law. The entire discount methodology in every case surveyed is an estimate of what it would cost, in time and money, to force a sale under the domestic partition statute that actually governs the property. An HUF coparcener’s only severance route is a partition suit under Indian civil procedure — a two-stage process (a preliminary decree establishing shares, then a final decree, often after a court-appointed commissioner’s survey, physically dividing the property or ordering a sale) under Order XX Rule 18 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, a mechanism described consistently across multiple independent Indian legal-practice sources (Khanna & Associates, Drishti Judiciary, Aran Law Firm, sjainlaw.in) but not read here at the bare-act text itself. No US Tax Court opinion or IRS position found has ever had to price the cost and delay of a foreign partition procedure as the input to a fractional interest discount — every case in the lineage above prices a partition remedy the court itself administers and already has cost data for from other cases. A US-qualified appraiser applying the Ludwick-style weighted model to an HUF interest would have no comparable body of domestic precedent on Indian partition-suit timelines and costs to draw on, and no source found has attempted to build one.

A domestic Indian precedent that doesn’t answer the US question

3. Indian courts have their own “discount for fractional interest in co-ownership” concept — a different tax, a different standard, not independently verified in full

A search for the discount concept applied to a coparcener’s share surfaced a 1987 Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (Allahabad) ruling indexed as 1987 (3) TMI 140, under India’s now-repealed Wealth-tax Act, whose own case summary is captioned “Discount for fractional interest in co-ownership.” A secondary synthesis of the ruling (a Google AI Overview built from the same indexing service’s public summary, not the tribunal’s full text) describes its core holding as: a fractional interest in jointly held property cannot be valued at its mathematically proportionate share of the whole’s market value, because a willing buyer would discount for litigation risk, possession difficulty, and lack of sole control. The tribunal’s full text sits behind a login wall on the indexing service (taxtmi.com) and was not read — consistent with this project’s rule that a stated credential gate is a legitimate stop, not something to route around. What can be said without the full text: an Indian court, applying India’s own now-repealed wealth tax and its own market-value standard, independently arrived at a discount concept for a coparcener’s fractional interest decades before this question was ever asked in a US context. That is suggestive that the underlying economic argument (illiquidity, lack of control, partition uncertainty) transfers across legal systems — it is not evidence about how a US court, US Treasury regulation, or US-qualified appraiser would or should treat the same interest under 26 CFR 20.2031-1(b), and this page does not claim otherwise.

4. Falsification: what was checked before this was written

Browser-Google searches combining the fractional/undivided interest discount doctrine’s own vocabulary with HUF, coparcenary, and Hindu Undivided Family terms (“fractional interest discount” “Hindu Undivided Family,” “undivided interest discount” coparcener estate tax, “cost of partition” OR “partition suit” HUF coparcenary “valuation discount” US estate) returned no matching content connecting the two literatures at all — results split cleanly into either US real-estate valuation-discount case law (Tax Adviser, Business Valuation Resources, NAEPC, Greenleaf Trust, Bessemer Trust, Pluris Valuation Advisors) or Indian domestic HUF-partition procedure and tax content (EIRC of ICAI, N D Savla & Associates, Sheokand Legal, Aran Law Firm), never both. Sherayzen Law Office’s HUF classification article (this series’ own Part 6 competitor, re-checked here) discusses Form 3520/5471/8865 filing consequences but not estate-tax valuation of the interest at all. The Chambers and Partners “Private Wealth 2026 — India” global practice guide (read in full; last updated 11 August 2026) covers US estate tax exposure for Indian families, coparcenary succession under the Hindu Succession Act, and discretionary-trust planning to avoid a future Indian estate tax, in three separate sections — but never connects coparcenary property to US valuation-discount mechanics. No competitor content of any kind, US or Indian, addressing the specific intersection this page raises was found.

Questions worth putting to a preparer or appraiser

  1. If a decedent’s estate includes an HUF coparcenary interest, has the appraiser been asked to establish the interest’s percentage as of the valuation date, and how that percentage would be defended against the fact that coparcenary membership is not fixed the way a tenancy-in-common deed is?
  2. Has any valuation of the interest applied a discount for lack of control and marketability consistent with the fractional interest discount case law — and if so, what partition-cost and partition-delay assumptions were used, and were they modeled against Indian civil procedure (Order XX Rule 18 CPC) or borrowed from US domestic partition-cost data that has no bearing on an Indian court?
  3. Is there a family partition deed, a standing family settlement, or any agreement analogous to the Tenancy in Common Agreement in Ludwick that would remove or bound the right to force a partition — a fact that case shows can be decisive to the size of the discount?
  4. Has the appraiser or preparer looked at Indian wealth-tax-era case law (now a dead body of law domestically, since the Wealth-tax Act was repealed) for any transferable valuation methodology, understanding that its persuasive value in a US Tax Court or IRS context has not been tested and is not asserted here?
