The tax election that can cost you the green card

A returning green-card holder can use the India–US treaty to file as a US nonresident and stop worldwide taxation. The same regulation that permits this says, in its own text, that filing that way may be used against you at the border. Almost nothing written about the DTAA tie-breaker for NRIs says so.

Sources read in full · US–India treaty TIAS 90-1218, Articles 1 & 4 · Treasury Technical Explanation · 26 CFR 301.7701(b)-7

Every guide to the India–US treaty tie-breaker (Article 4) explains the four tests — permanent home, centre of vital interests, habitual abode, nationality — and says a green-card holder who loses US residency under them can file as a nonresident and stop US tax on Indian-source income. That much is correct, and several competent, primary-source-citing articles already say it. What none of them say is that the regulation making this election legal, 26 CFR 301.7701(b)-7, contains its own sentence warning that the filing itself can be read by immigration authorities as evidence against the green card. This page is about that sentence, and about the two other conditions on the election that get treated as settled when they are not quite what they seem.

The one nobody quotes

1. The election is legal, filed on Form 1040NR — and the regulation says filing it that way “may affect” your green card

A green-card holder who is a dual resident under the treaty, and who wins the Article 4(2) tie-breaker in India’s favour, may elect to be taxed as a nonresident for the year. The mechanics are in 26 CFR 301.7701(b)-7(b), which sets out the filing requirement:

26 CFR 301.7701(b)-7(b), Filing requirements: “An alien individual described in paragraph (a) of this section who determines his or her U.S. tax liability as if he or she were a nonresident alien shall make a return on Form 1040NR… The individual shall attach a statement… [Form 8833]… The filing of a Form 1040NR by an individual described in paragraph (a) of this section may affect the determination by the Immigration and Naturalization Service as to whether the individual qualifies to maintain a residency permit.”

That sentence is in the regulation itself, not a lawyer’s inference from it. Every article found on this topic — including two written specifically about green-card holders using the DTAA tie-breaker — discusses the tax mechanics (Form 8833, the $1,000 penalty under §6712 for not filing it) and, separately, the immigration mechanics (the 6-month and 12-month absence rules, Form I-407, re-entry permits) as two unrelated compliance checklists. None found connects them: the regulation that lets you file as a tax nonresident says, in the same subsection, that the act of doing so is itself an input to whether USCIS lets you keep the card — independent of how many days you were physically absent.

2. Why this even works for a green-card holder but not for a citizen

The reason a treaty tie-breaker can rescue a green-card holder from US tax, but never a citizen, is written into the saving clause the first page of this series already found gutting Article 20 for pensions. The same clause draws the citizen/resident line here too — but on different terms than most guides imply.

Article 1(3), the saving clause: “… a Contracting State may tax its residents (as determined under Article 4 (Residence)), and by reason of citizenship may tax its citizens, as if the Convention had not come into effect.”

Read the parenthetical literally: residency for saving-clause purposes is not raw US domestic law — it is residency as determined under Article 4, after the tie-breaker runs. The Treasury Technical Explanation removes any doubt with a worked example built for exactly this fact pattern:

Technical Explanation to Article 1: “… if an individual who is not a U.S. citizen is a resident of the United States under the Code, and is also a resident of India under Indian law, and that individual has a permanent home available to him in India and not in the United States, he would be treated as a resident of India under Article 4 and this determination would apply for purposes of the saving clause. The United States would not be permitted to apply its statutory rules to that person if they are inconsistent with the treaty.”

That is the green-card holder’s entire case, stated by the US Treasury itself. But the same paragraph 3 has a second, independent branch — citizenship — that has nothing to do with Article 4. A US citizen who also wins the Article 4(2) tie-breaker for India is irrelevant to the saving clause: the citizenship branch taxes them “as if the Convention had not come into effect” regardless of where the tie-breaker lands. Article 1(4)(a), the list of provisions the saving clause does not override, was already read in full for page 1 of this series: it names paragraph 2 of Article 9, paragraphs 2 and 6 of Article 20, and Articles 25, 26 and 27. Article 4 itself is not on that list. For a citizen, the tie-breaker changes nothing about US taxation directly — its only effect runs through Article 25’s foreign-tax-credit mechanism, not through any reduction of the US filing obligation. Guides that describe the tie-breaker as if it works the same way for citizens and green-card holders are describing two different mechanisms with one sentence.

The scope limit almost nobody states

3. The election only relieves income tax liability — not CFC, not PFIC-adjacent status, not the rest of the Code

Even for the green-card holder for whom the election legitimately works, its reach is narrower than “I am now a nonresident.” The same regulation limits it in its own next paragraph:

26 CFR 301.7701(b)-7(a)(3), Other Code purposes: “Generally, for purposes of the Internal Revenue Code other than the computation of the individual’s United States income tax liability, the individual shall be treated as a United States resident. Therefore, for example, the individual shall be treated as a United States resident for purposes of determining whether a foreign corporation is a controlled foreign corporation under section 957 or whether a foreign corporation is a foreign personal holding company under section 552.”

The election is a computation rule for one number — the income tax liability line — not a general change of status. It was not tested in this pass against §1298’s PFIC shareholder-counting rules directly (the regulation names §957 and §552 as its own examples, not §1298), so whether an Indian mutual fund holding still counts this person as a US person for PFIC purposes after the election is not confirmed here and is flagged as the open question rather than assumed either way. What is confirmed is the general principle: the treaty election is narrower than it sounds, and a preparer treating it as a blanket nonresident status for every purpose is extending it further than the regulation itself does.

4. The India side of this is well covered elsewhere — deliberately not re-derived here

India’s own residency mechanics — the Section 6 day-count tests, the RNOR window under Section 6(6), the 60-day/120-day/182-day substitutions for citizens leaving for employment — are accurately and thoroughly covered by existing practitioner content (this page does not attempt to improve on that coverage, only to note it exists). The gap this page addresses sits entirely on the US primary-source side: what the saving clause actually does to the tie-breaker’s effect, and what the election regulation says about its own scope and its own immigration-adjacent risk.

Questions worth putting to a preparer

  1. Am I a US citizen or a green-card holder? The saving clause treats these two cases differently at the level of the treaty text, not just as a matter of degree.
  2. If I am a green-card holder claiming a nonresident position under Article 4(2), has Form 8833 been filed with the Form 1040NR, and has someone raised — even to rule it out — whether this filing pattern is a factor in any pending or future USCIS abandonment determination?
  3. Is the nonresident election being applied only to income tax liability, or is it being assumed to also change my status for CFC, FPHC, or other Code purposes it does not reach by its own terms?
  4. If I am a citizen, is the DTAA tie-breaker outcome being relied on for anything beyond Article 25 foreign-tax-credit ordering? It has no independent effect on US worldwide taxation for a citizen.

This is not tax or immigration advice. It is a reading of primary documents, published so that the questions can be asked. It creates no professional relationship, computes no tax, and should not be relied on for a filing position or an immigration decision.
