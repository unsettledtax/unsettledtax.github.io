Section 2014’s foreign death tax credit cannot reach a rupee of your Indian estate

A US citizen or green-card holder’s worldwide estate includes every Indian asset at fair market value on the day they die. The one credit built to offset double taxation on exactly that — 26 U.S.C. §2014 — is defined around a foreign tax “actually paid.” India abolished its estate duty in 1985. The credit is not reduced. It is structurally zero, every time, for every Indian asset, and popular guidance states the two facts on either side of that sentence without ever writing the sentence.

Every competent NRI estate-planning article gets the two headline facts right: a US citizen or green-card holder’s taxable estate is worldwide, and India has had no estate or inheritance tax since the Estate Duty Act was repealed in 1985. What none of them do is run those two facts through the one Code section that exists specifically to prevent a worldwide estate from being taxed twice on the same foreign asset. That section doesn’t just fail to help here — its own text guarantees it cannot help, for a reason that has nothing to do with India’s absent treaty and everything to do with what §2014 requires before it activates at all.

The mechanism no competitor states

1. Section 2014 credits foreign tax “actually paid.” India has none to pay.

A US citizen or green-card holder’s gross estate is defined without any situs limitation:

26 U.S.C. §2031(a): “The value of the gross estate of the decedent shall be determined by including… the value at the time of his death of all property, real or personal, tangible or intangible, wherever situated.”

26 U.S.C. §2033: “The value of the gross estate shall include the value of all property to the extent of the interest therein of the decedent at the time of his death.”

That reaches an Indian flat, an NRE fixed deposit, Indian mutual fund units, an HUF coparcenary interest — everything, at fair market value, with no carve-out for the asset’s location. (Foreign real property was excluded once, for decedents dying before 1 July 1964 under a transitional rule in 26 CFR 20.2031-1(a); Congress closed that window over sixty years ago. It is not available now.) The mechanism built to prevent double taxation on that worldwide reach is the foreign death tax credit:

26 U.S.C. §2014(a): “The tax imposed by section 2001 shall be credited with the amount of any estate, inheritance, legacy, or succession taxes actually paid to any foreign country in respect of any property situated within such foreign country and included in the gross estate.”

The credit is not a percentage, a cap, or a treaty benefit that India happens to lack — it is arithmetically tied to an amount of tax actually paid to India. Since 1985, that amount is always zero, for every decedent, on every Indian asset, regardless of value. §2014(d) requires the taxpayer to establish “the amount of taxes actually paid to the foreign country” and “the amount and date of each payment thereof” before any credit is allowed at all — there is nothing to establish. This is a distinct, narrower point than “India has no estate tax treaty with the US,” which is the farthest every competitor found goes. A treaty, if one existed, could only ever operate on top of §2014’s own actually-paid requirement (the 15 countries with a US estate tax treaty, per the IRS’s own list below, still generally have their own estate or inheritance tax for the credit to attach to). India’s absence from that list is a second, independent reason relief is unavailable — not the only reason, and not the one that would apply even in a hypothetical world where a US-India estate tax treaty existed.

2. What the IRS's own list confirms, read directly

The IRS publishes the complete list of countries with an estate and/or gift tax treaty with the United States. Read directly at irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/ estate-gift-tax-treaties-international: fifteen countries — Australia, Austria, Canada, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Netherlands, South Africa, Switzerland, United Kingdom. India is not on it. This corroborates, with the primary document itself rather than a secondary paraphrase, what the strongest competitor content already states correctly. The gap this page is built on is not that fact — it is the mechanical reason, inside §2014’s own text, that a treaty would only ever be a second layer on top of an already-zero credit for India specifically, since India has no underlying tax for any treaty to reference.

A second, unresolved question this page does not answer

3. What is a coparcener's HUF interest worth under the willing-buyer/willing-seller standard?

Section 2033’s “interest therein” language is broad enough to reach an HUF coparcener’s undivided birthright share in joint family property (the entity this family’s own part 6 examined for US classification purposes). Valuation is governed by:

26 CFR 20.2031-1(b): “The fair market value is the price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or to sell and both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts.”

A coparcener cannot sell their share to a willing buyer the way an owner of a fee-simple flat can — Hindu law makes the interest severable only through partition, and the body of coparceners (and therefore the size of any given member’s share) fluctuates by birth and death within the family, not by market transaction. A search combining the regulation’s own valuation language with HUF/coparcenary terms returned no content connecting the two at all. This page does not resolve how such an interest should be valued — it flags, as a distinct open question from the §2014 finding above, that the standard FMV test was not built with this kind of ownership in mind, and no primary guidance addressing the mismatch was found in this pass.

Questions worth putting to a preparer

  1. Has the estate’s exposure been modelled as worldwide (citizen/green-card domicile) or US-situs-only (non-domiciled)— and is that domicile determination current, not assumed from citizenship or visa status alone?
  2. For any Indian asset in the estate, has a foreign death tax credit under §2014 actually been claimed or assumed available — and if so, what Indian tax payment supports it, given India has had no estate duty since 1985?
  3. Does the estate include any HUF coparcenary interest, and if so, how was it valued for Form 706 purposes given that it cannot be sold to a willing buyer the way fee-simple property can?
  4. Has a life insurance policy, gifting program, or other exposure-reduction strategy been modelled specifically against the fact that no treaty and no credit will reduce the US tax on Indian assets, unlike a comparable UK- or Japan-domiciled estate?
  5. Is the valuation date and FX conversion for Indian real estate and financial accounts documented with a qualified appraisal, given US financial institutions and the IRS will require it before releasing a transfer certificate?
