Indian EPF, T1135, and Article 18: What the Canadian Statute and Treaty Actually Say

This is the first Canada-vertical page in this series: the reader is a Canadian tax resident with an Indian Employees' Provident Fund or Public Provident Fund balance. Two questions dominate the popular guidance — does the fund have to be reported on Form T1135, and does the India-Canada tax treaty shield the eventual withdrawal? A closed regulatory list nobody has cited answers the first question uncontestably. The treaty text, read in full, answers the second in the opposite direction from the most-visited generalist guide — and shows that even the strongest specialist competitor in this space contradicts itself on the identical provision.

Roughly 1.86 million people of Indian origin live in Canada (2021 Census, StatCan) — essentially tied with the UK's Indian-origin population this series covered in Parts 14–15, and built on the same skill: read the primary source in full, quote it verbatim, and show precisely where the popular paraphrase diverges. Canada's EPF/PPF guidance ecosystem is more sophisticated than the UK's — one specialist firm, TrustNRI, gets the core treaty rule right on its own dedicated page. That same firm's other page, reviewed by a chartered accountant, gets it wrong. Both cannot be correct, and neither cites the one regulation that actually closes the T1135 question.

1. The uncontested chain: EPF/PPF cannot escape T1135 via the "exempt trust" route

Form T1135 (Foreign Income Verification Statement) is required of a Canadian resident whose aggregate cost amount of "specified foreign property" exceeds CAD 100,000. An Indian EPF or PPF account is foreign property. The natural taxpayer-favourable argument is that a retirement fund should be carved out the way a US IRA or 401(k) effectively is — and Canada's Income Tax Act does contain exactly such a carve-out. Read in full at laws-lois.justice.gc.ca, the chain runs through four provisions, each quoted verbatim below.

Income Tax Act s.233.3(1), definition of "specified foreign property," paragraph (n) excludes from the reporting requirement:

"an interest in a trust that is described in paragraph (a) or (b) of the definition exempt trust in subsection 233.2(1)…"

s.233.2(1), "exempt trust," paragraph (a) — the route this page tests — reads:

"a trust that is governed by a foreign retirement arrangement;"

s.248(1), the Act's general definitions section, defines the term that paragraph (a) turns on:

"foreign retirement arrangement means a prescribed plan or arrangement;"

"Prescribed" sends the question to the regulations. There is exactly one prescription, and it is narrow. Income Tax Regulation 6803, read in full:

"For the purposes of the definition foreign retirement arrangement in subsection 248(1) of the Act, a prescribed plan or arrangement is a plan or arrangement to which subsection 408(a), (b) or (h) of the United States' Internal Revenue Code of 1986, as amended from time to time, applies."

What the closed list actually names

Only US IRC §408 plans. Nothing Indian, nothing generic.

Regulation 6803 is a closed list with one entry: plans governed by IRC §408(a), (b) or (h) — the US statutory provisions for Individual Retirement Accounts. It names no other country's retirement regime, and contains no general test ("a plan recognized as a retirement arrangement by a foreign government" or similar) that EPF or PPF could plausibly satisfy on its own facts. The s.233.2(1)(a) exempt-trust route is a US-IRA carve-out in substance, whatever its general-sounding label suggests. EPF and PPF cannot reach it. This closes off, on the plain statutory text, the intuitively plausible argument that a retirement fund should not have to be reported the way an ordinary foreign bank account does.

The alternative exempt-trust route, s.233.2(1)(b) — a general test for a foreign income-tax-exempt pension trust — also plausibly fails on its own terms: it requires the trust be either "maintained primarily for the benefit of non-resident individuals" or "governed by an employees profit sharing plan," and EPF is neither (it is maintained primarily for Indian-resident contributors). That second route was not chased to a definitive conclusion this session and is carried as INFERENCE, not because the (a) route leaves any doubt but because a complete negative proof requires ruling out every alternative route, and (b) was reasoned from the statutory text rather than confirmed against a CRA ruling on point.

TrustNRI's pension page correctly states the bottom-line consequence — that EPF still counts toward the CAD 100,000 T1135 threshold "even if the pension itself is Canada-exempt" — but does not explain why via this specific mechanism. No competitor guide found in this session cites Regulation 6803 or the exempt-trust definition chain at all. The conclusion is not new; the statutory reasoning foreclosing the alternative appears to be.

2. Article 18: the treaty runs source-state, not residence-state

The India-Canada DTAA (1996) contains exactly one pension provision. Read in full at treaty-accord.gc.ca — and confirmed by a full-text search of all 30 articles for "pension," "annuity," "lump sum," and "periodic," which returns matches only in Article 18 itself — Article 18, "Pensions," in its complete two-sentence entirety:

"Pensions arising in a Contracting State shall be taxable only in that State. Pensions shall be deemed to arise in a Contracting State when the payer is that State itself, a political subdivision, a local authority or a resident of that State."

This is a source-state-exclusive rule. A pension paid by an Indian payer to a Canadian resident is taxable only in India — the opposite of a rule that would let the residence state (Canada) tax it. Adjacent Articles 19 (Government Service) and 20 (Students and Apprentices, a single unnumbered paragraph) were read in full to confirm there is no secondary pension provision elsewhere in the treaty's numbering.

3. Finding: the top generalist guide states Article 18 backwards

countrytaxcalc.com's "India to Canada Tax Guide 2026" states, quoted verbatim:

"Pensions: taxable only in the country of residence (Canadian pension paid to India resident = taxable in India only; Indian pension paid to Canadian resident = taxable in Canada only)."

and in its FAQ:

"The India-Canada DTAA (1996) contains a pension article that taxes pension income only in the country of residence… For EPF and PPF: the treaty's pension article potentially covers EPF as a social security scheme — potentially deferring Canadian tax until withdrawal."

Both statements describe Article 18 as a residence-state rule. The treaty text says the opposite: a source-state rule. countrytaxcalc's own worked example — "Indian pension paid to Canadian resident = taxable in Canada only" — is the direct inverse of what Article 18 actually provides, which on these facts is taxable in India only. This is not an omitted nuance; it is a directionally reversed statement of a two-sentence, unambiguous treaty rule, discoverable only by reading the treaty text rather than trusting the summary — the same shape of finding as the UK vertical's Finance Act 2017 Schedule 3 case in Part 14.

4. Finding: the strongest competitor contradicts itself across its own two pages

TrustNRI is the single strongest India-Canada specialist guide found in this search. Its dedicated pension page (situations/canada-indian-pension-epf-nps-tax) states Article 18 correctly and precisely, quoted verbatim:

"Under Article 18 of the India-Canada treaty, a pension arising in India, one paid by an Indian payer such as your former Indian employer, the provident fund authority or an Indian insurer, is taxable only in India. Unlike most treaties, which give the country of residence the sole right, this one gives it to the source country, India."

This page also correctly and honestly flags the open question this page returns to in Section 5: whether an EPF lump-sum withdrawal counts as a treaty "pension" at all, calling it "not perfectly settled… fact-specific and worth confirming before you withdraw."

But TrustNRI's own T1135/FATCA blog post (blog/canada-nri-t1135-guide, dated 5 April 2026, byline "By Vipul Sharma, Founder… Reviewed by Preetesh Maloo, Chartered Accountant, NRI Tax Partner"), discussing the mirror-image case of a Canadian pension paid to a returning India resident, states:

"The India-Canada DTAA Article 20 (pensions), and specifically Article 20(2) for RRSP/RRIF income, gives the residence state primary taxing rights, with source-state credit available."

A CA-reviewed page citing a treaty article that does not exist

Article 20 is "Students and Apprentices." There is no Article 20(2).

Checked directly against the treaty text read in Section 2: Article 20 of the India-Canada DTAA is titled "Students and Apprentices," is a single unnumbered paragraph, and has no subsection (2). No article of the treaty mentions RRSPs, RRIFs, or annuities by name at all — a full-text search confirms it. The blog post's claim is wrong on the article number, wrong on the (nonexistent) subsection, and wrong on the underlying mechanism it attributes to that subsection (residence-state-with-credit, versus Article 18's actual source-state-exclusive rule) — all on the identical treaty provision TrustNRI's own pension page states correctly. This is not a difference in interpretation; it is a citation to text that is not in the treaty, carrying a chartered-accountant review byline, on the page most likely to be read by someone specifically researching T1135 compliance rather than pension taxation.

5. The open question no ranking guide resolves: is a lump-sum EPF withdrawal a treaty "pension" at all?

The India-Canada DTAA never defines "pension." Confirmed by the same full-text search noted in Section 2: the words "annuity," "lump sum," and "periodic" appear nowhere in the treaty's 30 articles. Article 18 simply says "pensions arising in a Contracting State" without saying what counts as one — whether a one-time provident-fund withdrawal is a "pension" in the treaty sense, or whether it is better characterized as ordinary income Canada can tax on its own domestic worldwide-income basis regardless of Article 18's source-state rule, is not settled by the treaty text itself.

The family's signature structure, repeated a third time

A genuine gap in the primary source, not merely unread guidance

This is structurally identical to two questions already carried open elsewhere in this series. Part 8 (the US vertical) found that the US-India treaty's Article 20(3) periodic-payment language does not plainly cover a one-time EPF lump sum either, leaving the same periodic-vs-lump-sum ambiguity unresolved under a different treaty's wording. Part 15 (the UK vertical) found that whether EPF qualifies as an "overseas pension scheme" under UK statute is undetermined because HMRC's own regulator-recognition test has no clear answer for a scheme that regulates itself. Here, the ambiguity is even more basic: the treaty text supplies no definition of "pension" to test EPF against at all. Three verticals, three different statutory mechanisms, the same shape of open question — which is either a coincidence of how lump-sum provident funds sit awkwardly across every bilateral tax treaty this family has examined, or a sign that "is a provident-fund lump sum a treaty pension" is simply an unresolved category of question in cross-border tax practice generally.

A live, real-world instance of exactly this uncertainty: a dated r/nri Reddit thread describes a Canadian tax resident whose EPF-withdrawal Article 18 treaty claim (Line 25600 of their return) is under active CRA Pre-assessment Review, with CRA requesting supporting documentation. This is cited here as an anecdote, not authority — a single Reddit post is not evidence of CRA's legal position, and the thread's outcome was not observed. It is included because it corroborates, from a real taxpayer's live experience, that this is not an abstract question: practitioners and CRA reviewers are actively working through it right now, on the identical treaty provision this page examines.

6. Where the popular guidance gets it right: the arrival-date FMV step-up

Not every angle checked this session produced an error. Income Tax Act s.128.1(1), read in full, deems a person becoming a Canadian resident to have disposed of most property immediately before arrival and reacquired it at that moment — the "deemed disposition" and "deemed acquisition" paragraphs, quoted verbatim:

"(b) the taxpayer is deemed to have disposed… of each property owned by the taxpayer… for proceeds equal to its fair market value at the time of disposition; … (c) the taxpayer shall be deemed to have acquired at the particular time each property deemed by paragraph 128.1(1)(b) to have been disposed of by the taxpayer, at a cost equal to the proceeds of disposition of the property."

In plain terms: for Canadian tax purposes, the cost base of most foreign assets — including, per both competitors checked, an EPF/PPF balance — resets to fair market value on the date Canadian residency begins, so only post-arrival growth is exposed to Canadian tax on eventual disposition or withdrawal. Both countrytaxcalc.com and TrustNRI's dedicated deemed-acquisition page state this correctly. TrustNRI's explanation in particular, with a worked example distinguishing the Canada-side FMV reset from the untouched India-side cost basis, is precise and did not turn up an error in this session's check. It is worth saying plainly: on this specific mechanic, the competitor guidance is right, and a reader following it here would not be misled.

7. What is verified and what is not

  • VERIFIED — Income Tax Act ss.233.3(1)(n), 233.2(1)(a), 248(1) and Income Tax Regulation 6803, read verbatim at laws-lois.justice.gc.ca: the "exempt trust" T1135 carve-out's "foreign retirement arrangement" prescription names only US IRC §408(a)/(b)/(h) plans.
  • VERIFIED — India-Canada DTAA (1996) Article 18, read in full at treaty-accord.gc.ca: a source-state-exclusive pension rule, confirmed by full-text search to be the treaty's sole pension provision.
  • VERIFIED — countrytaxcalc.com's guide states Article 18 backwards, as a residence-state rule, inverting its own worked example's correct answer.
  • VERIFIED — TrustNRI's pension page states Article 18 correctly; its separately published, CA-reviewed T1135 blog post cites a nonexistent "Article 20(2)" and misstates the taxing-rights mechanism on the identical provision.
  • VERIFIED — Income Tax Act s.128.1(1), read in full: the deemed-disposition/ deemed-acquisition FMV step-up on becoming Canadian resident; both competitors checked state this mechanic correctly.
  • INFERENCE — that the s.233.2(1)(b) alternative exempt-trust route also fails for EPF/PPF; reasoned from the statutory text (the "non-resident individuals" / "employees profit sharing plan" sub-conditions) but not confirmed against a CRA ruling on point.
  • INFERENCE — the Indian-side exempt-exempt-exempt characterization of EPF (s.80C relief in, ss.10(11)/10(12) exemption out) that all sources describing the fact pattern assume; carried from the Indian statute as background, not independently re-read at primary level for this Canada page.
  • Not resolved — whether an EPF lump-sum withdrawal is a treaty "pension" under Article 18 at all, given the treaty defines no such term. No CRA technical interpretation, Tax Court or Federal Court of Appeal decision on point was found. The Reddit CRA Pre-assessment Review thread is a live signal that this question is genuinely contested in practice, cited as anecdote only.

Questions worth putting to a CPA or Canadian tax lawyer before filing

  1. Does my EPF or PPF holding need to be reported on Form T1135 this year given the CAD 100,000 aggregate cost-amount threshold across all specified foreign property — and on what specific statutory basis, if any, would you argue it does not, given Regulation 6803's closed list?
  2. On what basis would you characterize an EPF lump-sum withdrawal as a treaty "pension" under Article 18 versus ordinary foreign income taxable in Canada under domestic rules regardless of the treaty — and how would you document that position if CRA opens a Pre-assessment Review on it, as has happened to at least one other filer?
  3. What is my Canadian arrival-date fair market value for this holding under s.128.1(1), and what documentation would support that value if CRA later asks for it?
