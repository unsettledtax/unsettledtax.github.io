India’s April–March tax year doesn’t automatically become a single US tax year on Form 1116

Every good Form 1116 guide tells you to gather your Indian TDS and advance tax for “the year.” None of them says which year — India’s, which runs April to March, or the US calendar year the credit actually gets claimed in. The regulation that answers this has an irrevocable election buried in it that no guide found mentions at all.

Sources read in full · 26 CFR 1.905-1 · 26 U.S. Code §905

This page is for a US citizen or green-card holder who is also an Indian taxpayer paying tax through the year — TDS withheld from salary or an NRO deposit month by month, quarterly advance-tax installments, and a final self-assessment payment when the Indian return is filed. All of that happens on India’s April–March fiscal year. Form 1116 runs on the US calendar year. The guides are excellent at telling you how to size the credit. None of them tell you which US tax year an installment paid in, say, February belongs to — or that the answer depends on an accounting-method election you make once, for life.

The one most often missed

1. The default rule is cash basis: credit falls in the US year you actually paid the Indian tax — not the year the income was earned, and not the Indian assessment year

Most individuals never make an election and file on the cash method. For a cash-method taxpayer, the regulation is direct:

26 CFR 1.905-1(c)(1): “Except as provided in paragraph (e) of this section, a taxpayer who uses the cash method of accounting may claim a foreign tax credit only in the taxable year in which the foreign income taxes are paid. Generally, foreign income taxes are considered paid in the taxable year in which the taxes are remitted to the foreign country.”

Read against India’s own payment calendar, this splits a single Indian tax year across two US returns as a matter of course, not as an edge case. The four advance-tax installments fall on 15 June, 15 September, 15 December, and 15 March. The June, September, and December installments land inside one US calendar year. The March installment, and any self-assessment tax paid when the return is filed (typically by 31 July, sometimes later), land in the next US calendar year — even though every one of those payments relates to the same Indian financial year and the same Indian income. A cash-method filer who gathers “this year’s Form 26AS” and puts the whole figure on one Form 1116 is using the Indian assessment year as the organizing unit. The regulation uses the date money left the taxpayer’s account instead.

2. The accrual election takes the opposite position — and it can’t be undone

Section 905(a) offers a way out of the payment-date scramble: elect to claim the credit on the accrual basis instead, regardless of the taxpayer’s regular accounting method for everything else.

26 U.S. Code §905(a): “The credits provided in this subpart may, at the option of the taxpayer and irrespective of the method of accounting employed in keeping his books, be taken in the year in which the taxes of the foreign country … accrued… If the taxpayer elects to take such credits in the year in which the taxes of the foreign country … accrued, the credits for all subsequent years shall be taken on the same basis”

That last clause is the trap: this is a once-only, all-future-years election. There is no switching back to cash-basis crediting in a later year because it happened to be more favorable. The regulation then defines exactly when a fiscal-year foreign tax “accrues,” using a fact pattern that is, in substance, the Indian case:

26 CFR 1.905-1(d)(6)(i), Example 1: “A, a U.S. citizen, resides and works in Country X… Country X has a tax year that begins on April 1 and ends on March 31. A’s wages are subject to net income tax… and are subject to withholding on a monthly basis by A’s employer… Under paragraph (d)(1) of this section, A’s liability for Country X net income tax accrues on March 31, Year 2, the last day of the Country X taxable year. The Country X net income tax withheld by A’s employer from A’s wages is a reasonable approximation of, and represents an advance payment of, A’s final net income tax liability for the year, which becomes fixed and determinable only at the close of the Country X taxable year. Thus, A cannot claim a credit for any portion of the Country X net income tax on A’s Federal income tax return for Year 1, and may claim a credit for the entire Country X net income tax that accrues on March 31, Year 2, on A’s Federal income tax return for Year 2.”

Country X’s April–March tax year in the regulation’s own worked example is India’s tax year. Under the accrual election, none of the monthly TDS or quarterly advance-tax installments is separately creditable as it is paid — each is treated as an “advance payment” of a liability that is not “fixed and determinable” until 31 March, and the entire year’s Indian tax becomes creditable in a single US tax year: the one in which that 31 March falls. That is the opposite of the cash-basis result in (1), where the same installments are split across two US years by payment date.

Why the choice is not cosmetic

3. The two methods can produce different credit amounts, not just different paperwork

Section 904 caps the credit at US tax on foreign-source income for the year, computed separately by category, and any excess only carries back one year or forward ten. A taxpayer whose Indian tax bill or US taxable income varies year to year — a large capital gain in one Indian financial year, a slow year the next — can find that bundling an entire Indian fiscal year’s tax into one US year under the accrual election pushes a chunk of otherwise-usable credit into the ten-year carryforward queue, where the cash-basis split across two smaller US-year claims might have absorbed more of it directly. Which result is better depends on the taxpayer’s specific income pattern in the straddle years and is not resolved by this page — the point here is narrower: the choice exists, it is irrevocable once made, and no India-facing guide found names it.

4. What the strongest India-facing guide gets right — and doesn’t reach

toolisky.com’s “Form 1116 for Indian Tax Paid: FTC Guide 2026” (read in full) is a genuinely thorough India-specific Form 1116 reference: it correctly covers the §901/904/27(a) statutory basis, the Form 67-to-Form 44 renumbering under the Income-tax Act, 2025, the PFIC interaction for Indian mutual funds, the NIIT gap Form 1116 doesn’t reach, exchange-rate conventions (“a consistently applied annual average” for recurring income), and two fully worked numeric examples with carryback/carryforward. Every worked example treats “Indian tax paid for the year” as a single figure pulled from Form 26AS or an AIS. At no point does it address which US tax year that figure belongs to when the underlying payments straddle India’s fiscal year, or that a taxpayer elects — permanently — between two different answers to that question. A dedicated search combining “Form 1116”, “India”, and either “accrual election” or “905(a)” returned no India-specific content connecting the two; the general cash-vs-accrual distinction for the foreign tax credit is documented (HTJ Tax, cloudexpattax.com) but not applied to India’s own fiscal-year calendar anywhere found. Separately, the popular India-side discourse on the calendar-year/financial-year mismatch (a 250-plus-comment r/IndiaTax thread and several shorter ones) is entirely about a different problem — reconciling Schedule FA’s calendar-year foreign-asset disclosure on the Indian return against financial-year income figures — not about which US year absorbs an Indian tax payment on Form 1116.

What this changes in practice

  • Before assuming “this year’s Indian tax” maps cleanly onto “this year’s Form 1116,” check whether the return has ever made a §905(a) accrual election. If not, the default is cash basis: each Indian payment is creditable in the US calendar year it was actually remitted, which will usually split a single Indian financial year’s TDS and advance tax across two US returns.
  • If an accrual election is being considered to simplify this, weigh it against §905(a)’s own irrevocability clause — it binds every future year, not just the one it’s made for.
  • The March 15 advance-tax installment and any self-assessment tax paid alongside the ITR are the payments most likely to fall into a different US tax year than the June/September/December installments for the same Indian income — the highest-risk items for a preparer defaulting to one figure per year.
  • This page does not resolve which method produces a larger usable credit in any specific case — that depends on the taxpayer’s year-to-year income and Indian tax pattern and needs to be modeled, not assumed.

Questions worth putting to a preparer

  1. Is this return on the cash or accrual method for foreign tax credit purposes specifically — has a §905(a) election ever been filed, and would it bind all future years?
  2. For the Indian tax being claimed this year, which specific payments (TDS dates, advance-tax installment dates, self-assessment date) is the figure built from, and do any of them fall in a different US calendar year than the others?
  3. If a March advance-tax installment or a post-year-end self-assessment payment was involved, has it been credited on the Form 1116 for the US year it was actually paid, or lumped in with the rest of the Indian financial year’s tax?
  4. Has the §904 category limitation been checked separately for each US year the Indian payments land in, rather than once for a single blended figure?
  5. Would modeling the accrual election change which US year absorbs the largest share of the credit — and is that trade-off worth an irrevocable election?
