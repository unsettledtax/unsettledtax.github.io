Selling inherited property in India, where the guides stop reading

FEMA’s repatriation route for an inherited property is not the same provision as the route for property an NRI bought themselves — and the Income-tax Act that governs the buyer’s TDS was renumbered on 1 April 2026, mid-way through the guidance still circulating about it. Read against the actual RBI directions, the actual statute, and IRC §1014, not the summaries.

Sources read in full · RBI FED Master Direction No. 12/2015-16 · RBI Master Circular on NRO Accounts, §6 · Income-tax Act, 2025, §393 · 26 U.S. Code §1014

Every competent guide to this topic gets the shape right: India taxes the sale, the US taxes the same sale on a different basis, the treaty and the Foreign Tax Credit keep you from paying twice, and a lower-deduction certificate saves you from a year of your own money sitting with the tax department. What follows is not a correction of that shape. It is what the primary documents say underneath it — the specific FEMA provision that actually governs an inherited property (not the one usually cited), the exact statutory clause behind the TDS obligation (now under a different section number than almost everything written about it), and the reason the US step-up in basis creates a documentation problem nobody prices in.

The one most often missed

1. Inherited property runs through a different FEMA provision than a self-purchased one

Almost every guide describes NRI property repatriation as one rule: sell, then remit up to USD 1 million a year. That figure is correct, but it conflates two provisions of RBI’s Master Direction that apply to different sellers.

FED Master Direction No. 12/2015-16, Part II, §1.2: “As per section 6(5) of FEMA, a person resident outside India can hold, own, transfer or invest in any immovable property situated in India if such property was acquired, held or owned by him/her when he/she was resident in India or inherited from a person resident in India.”

§8.1: “A person acquiring property in accordance with section 6(5) of FEMA… or his successor cannot repatriate outside India the sale proceeds of such immovable property without the prior permission of the Reserve Bank. However, if such a person is resident outside India, he/she can utilise the remittance facilities available under the Foreign Exchange Management (Remittance of Assets) Regulations, 2016.”

That is the inherited-property route: the default is that RBI’s prior permission is required, and the USD 1 million Remittance of Assets facility exists specifically as the substitute that avoids needing to ask. The provision usually quoted instead — §8.2, which allows repatriation for an NRI/OCI who bought the property themselves — requires proof the purchase was paid for in foreign exchange or from an FCNR(B)/NRE account. An inherited property was never bought by the heir at all, so §8.2’s conditions do not fit the fact pattern being described; §8.1 does.

2. The “maximum two residential properties” cap belongs to the other route

§8.2 of the same Master Direction adds a condition that gets carried into general commentary as if it were a universal NRI rule:

§8.2: “…in the case of residential property, the repatriation of sale proceeds is restricted to not more than two such properties.”

That sentence sits inside the list of conditions for the self-purchased-with-foreign-exchange route. The inherited-property route (§8.1, via the Remittance of Assets Regulations) is governed instead by RBI’s Master Circular on NRO Accounts, which states its own condition for the USD 1 million ceiling and does not repeat a two-property cap:

RBI Master Circular on NRO Accounts, §6.2(a): “NRI/PIO may remit an amount, not exceeding USD one million per financial year, out of the balances held in NRO accounts / sale proceeds of assets / the assets in India acquired by him by way of inheritance / legacy, on production of documentary evidence in support of acquisition, inheritance or legacy of assets by the remitter and… certificate be a Chartered Accountant in the formats prescribed by the Central Board of Direct Taxes… [A.P.(DIR Series) Circular No. 56 dated November 26, 2002].”

Read narrowly, not extended

Neither circular states in so many words that the two-property cap does not apply to inherited property — the cap simply appears only inside §8.2’s condition list, and §8.2 is not the provision the RBI directs an inherited-property seller to. Treat this as the primary text supports, not as a confirmed absence: an authorised dealer bank processing a fourth or fifth inherited property in one year is the fact pattern where this distinction would actually be tested, and no ruling on that specific case was found.

3. TDS is deducted on the entire sale price, and there is no ₹50 lakh floor for a non-resident seller

This part is described correctly by most competent guides — the mistake is in which section they cite. The Income-tax Act, 2025, in force since 1 April 2026, renumbered the provision entirely.

Income-tax Act, 2025, §393(2), Table, Sl. No. 17 (successor to §195 of the Income-tax Act, 1961): payee “Any non-resident (not being a company) or a foreign company”; payer “Any person”; nature of sum “Any interest… or any other sum chargeable under the provisions of this Act, not being income chargeable under the head ‘Salaries’”; rate “Rates in force”. No threshold limit is listed for this line.

Compare that to the line immediately above it in the same Act, governing a resident seller:

§393(1), Table, Sl. No. 3(i): consideration for transfer of immovable property, rate 1% of consideration or stamp duty value, threshold limit fifty lakh rupees.

The ₹50 lakh floor is a feature of the resident-seller provision only. The non-resident-seller line carries no threshold at all — a buyer purchasing a ₹10 lakh inherited flat from an NRI owes the same categorical TDS obligation as one buying a ₹10 crore property. And because “any other sum chargeable” is not defined as the capital gain, the statute’s own note removes any nexus requirement for the obligation itself:

§393(2), Note 3(b): “the obligation to deduct tax at source… extend[s] to all persons resident or non-resident, whether or not, the non-resident person has (i) a residence or place of business or business connection in India; or (ii) any other presence in any manner whatsoever in India.”

In practice, buyers and their conveyancing lawyers default to withholding on the full consideration at the applicable capital-gains rate, which is why the pre-sale lower or nil deduction certificate matters as much as it does — it is the only mechanism that substitutes an assessing officer’s computed figure for the buyer’s own estimate.

Cross-referenced, not read at the primary text

The lower-deduction certificate provision also moved

Two independent secondary sources (a CBDT section cross-reference table and a professional tax-advisory cross-reference) agree that §197 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 — the certificate for deduction at a lower rate — corresponds to §395 of the Income-tax Act, 2025. The bare statutory text of the new §395 could not be retrieved directly from incometaxindia.gov.in in this research pass (the section index at that domain returned an unrelated Companies Act provision under the same URL pattern). Treat the section number as well-corroborated but not independently confirmed against the primary text.

4. The US step-up in basis has no foreign-property carve-out in the statute — and no Form 706 to document it

US guides get the headline right: inherited property gets a stepped-up basis, so the US capital gain is usually far smaller than the Indian one measured from the original owner’s cost. Read the actual statute and a second issue appears that guides do not raise.

26 U.S. Code §1014(a): “Except as otherwise provided in this section, the basis of property in the hands of a person acquiring the property from a decedent… shall… be… the fair market value of the property at the date of the decedent’s death…”

Nothing in §1014(a) or the “property acquired from the decedent” definitions in §1014(b) distinguishes US-situs from foreign-situs property, or a US decedent from a foreign one. The step-up is unconditional on that dimension — which is exactly why it applies to an Indian flat inherited from an Indian-resident parent who never filed a US return.

The complication is §1014(f), added to prevent basis overstatement:

§1014(f)(1): the basis of estate property “shall not exceed… in the case of property the final value of which has been determined for purposes of the [estate] tax… such value, and… in the case of property… with respect to which a statement has been furnished under section 6035(a)… such value.”

That consistency rule exists because, for a US decedent’s estate, Form 706 and the Schedule A/6035 statement to heirs create an IRS-established figure the heir’s basis is pinned to. An NRI parent’s estate ordinarily has no Form 706, because the parent was not a US person and the property is not US-situs — so there is no government-established date-of-death value for the heir to inherit along with the property. The step-up under §1014(a) still applies in full; there is simply no official document proving the number. The burden of establishing fair market value at the date of death — typically an independent appraisal or comparable-sales valuation contemporaneous with the death, translated to dollars at that date’s exchange rate — sits entirely with the heir, discovered only if the position is ever examined.

General mechanism, not independently re-derived

The Foreign Tax Credit mismatch is real but not new

Because India taxes the gain from the original owner’s cost while the US taxes it from the stepped-up basis, the Indian tax bill is often much larger than the US tax on the same sale. Since the Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116) can only offset US tax actually due, a chunk of the Indian credit can go unused in the year of sale, carried back one year or forward ten under §904(c) rather than refunded. This mechanism is well-established international tax law and is described accurately by competent competitor guides; it is not re-derived from primary text in this page and is included for completeness, not as a new finding.

What this changes in practice

  • Ask which FEMA provision your repatriation is actually proceeding under — §8.1 via the Remittance of Assets Regulations (inherited property), or §8.2 (property you personally paid for in foreign exchange). The conditions, and possibly the two-property cap, differ.
  • Confirm your CA and the buyer’s deductor are citing the Income-tax Act, 2025 by its current section numbers (§393(2) for the TDS obligation), not the 1961 Act’s §195 — guidance still in wide circulation was written before 1 April 2026 and has not been updated.
  • Apply for the lower/nil deduction certificate before the sale closes, not after. This is the one step every competent guide gets right, and it is right.
  • Commission an independent date-of-death valuation for the property as soon as possible after inheriting it, specifically because no US estate tax filing will ever generate one for you.

Questions worth putting to a preparer

  1. Are you repatriating this under §8.1 (inherited, via Remittance of Assets Regulations, 2016) or §8.2 (self-purchased with foreign exchange)? The documentation and any property-count limits differ.
  2. Is the buyer’s TDS being computed on the full sale consideration by default, or has a lower/nil deduction certificate already been obtained?
  3. Are you citing the Income-tax Act, 2025 section numbers, or the 1961 Act’s?
  4. Do you have an independent, contemporaneous date-of-death valuation for the inherited property, translated to dollars at that date’s exchange rate — not a valuation obtained at the time of sale?
  5. Have you modelled whether this year’s Foreign Tax Credit fully absorbs the Indian tax paid, or whether a carryover is expected?
