The Temporary Repatriation Facility Trades Away Your India Tax Credit — HMRC Says So Directly

This is the fourth UK-vertical page. Part 18 found that a reader with NRE interest can often skip the FIG regime's 4-year window entirely and rely on the India-UK DTAA's tax-sparing credit instead. This page asks the harder question sitting behind it: for the years of Indian income already accrued before the reader arrived in the UK or before 6 April 2025 — NRO interest, mutual fund gains, rental income, sitting untouched in Indian accounts — does the Temporary Repatriation Facility's discounted flat rate actually beat ordinary remittance plus the DTAA credit? HMRC's own manual answers that question directly, and the answer is not the one most coverage implies.

New: Finance Act 2025, Schedule 10 read in full at legislation.gov.uk · HMRC's RDRM manual (RDRM71000, 73310, 73340, 75100) read in full · the designation-vs-remittance and mixed-fund mechanics quoted verbatim · four competitor/professional sources read in full and checked against the primary text

The remittance basis was abolished from 6 April 2025. In its place: the FIG (Foreign Income and Gains) regime, a 4-year exemption for new UK residents (Part 18's subject), and — for someone who already used the remittance basis before the change and is sitting on years of unremitted foreign income and gains — the Temporary Repatriation Facility. The pitch, repeated across nearly every piece of coverage found this session, is simple: pay a reduced flat rate now (12% for 2025-26 and 2026-27, rising to 15% for 2027-28) instead of full UK income tax or CGT rates later, and the money is clean to bring home. For a reader whose "foreign income" is specifically Indian income that has already had Indian tax withheld or paid on it, that pitch conceals a real choice with a real cost, one HMRC's own guidance states in plain terms and no competitor source read this session mentions at all.

1. What "designation" actually is — and it is not the same thing as remitting money

Finance Act 2025, Schedule 10, Part 1, read in full at legislation.gov.uk, creates the charge. Paragraph 1(1)–(2), quoted verbatim:

"This Part of this Schedule sets out a charge on amounts of qualifying overseas capital of individuals previously subject to the remittance basis…The charge is to be known as the temporary repatriation facility charge…referred to as the ‘TRF charge’."

The rate schedule, paragraph 1(8), quoted verbatim in full:

"(a) in the case of amounts of qualifying overseas capital designated in a return for the tax year 2025-26 or 2026-27, the amount equal to 12% of the amount of that capital, and (b) in the case of amounts of qualifying overseas capital designated in a return for the tax year 2027-28, the amount equal to 15% of the amount of that capital."

Paragraph 2(2) sets the eligibility condition that matters most for the mechanics: an amount of capital qualifies only if, among other conditions, "the amount has not been remitted to the United Kingdom." Designation is made through a formal election in a Self Assessment return (paragraph 8(1)), due within 12 months of the following 31 January. HMRC's RDRM73310, quoted verbatim, confirms the practical consequence popular coverage tends to blur:

"As an individual is not required to remit funds in order to designate them, for the purposes of calculating the TRF charge the exchange rate prevailing on the date of designation should be used…A designation is treated as having taken place at the beginning of the tax year…The exchange rate on 6 April should be used even if the individual remits the designated amounts during the tax year."

Designation and remittance are two separate acts. A reader can designate a pot of pre-2025 NRO interest or mutual fund gains, pay the 12–15% TRF charge on it this year, and leave the actual cash sitting in an Indian account for years afterward — or, in principle, never move it at all, subject to the wider Schedule 10 remittance-timing framework this page does not exhaustively map. The TRF charge is a tax election on a designated amount, not a transaction fee on a wire transfer.

2. Mixed funds: TRF capital jumps the queue, and the usual transaction-by-transaction rule is suspended

ITA 2007 s.809Q normally governs which slice of a "mixed fund" — an account blending several kinds of income, gains, and capital across years — is treated as remitted first when a transfer is made, ordinarily putting untaxed income ahead of capital. Schedule 10 paragraph 15 amends s.809Q directly, inserting a new priority step ahead of the existing ones. Quoted verbatim:

"Step A1 — Find the amount (if any) of income or capital of the individual for the relevant tax year in the mixed fund immediately before the transfer that is TRF capital. If the amount of the transfer is equal to, or less than, the amount of TRF capital, treat the transfer as containing only TRF capital. Otherwise…treat so much of the transfer as does not exceed the amount of the TRF capital as being comprised of TRF capital, and apply the following steps to the remainder…"

HMRC's RDRM75100 states the practical effect in plain English: designated TRF capital is "to be remitted to the UK in priority to all other kinds of income and gains in a mixed fund" — the reverse of the ordinary s.809Q order, which normally treats capital as the last thing remitted, not the first. RDRM75100 adds a second relaxation not widely covered: "During the TRF period an annualised basis applies to mixed funds that contain or contained TRF capital…instead of the usual transaction-by-transaction basis." For a reader whose Indian bank and mutual fund accounts genuinely blend pre-2025 and post-2025 income — the ordinary shape of an NRO or NRE account held for years — this is a real, favourable, statutory answer to the mixed-fund question, not a case where "ordinary cleansing law still applies." It is a specific, named exception, and it exists because Parliament wrote it in, not because HMRC discretion allows it.

3. The India-specific question this page exists to answer: is Indian tax already paid creditable against the TRF charge?

This is the mechanism most likely to matter in pounds and pence to this family's actual reader, and it is answered directly, not left ambiguous, by both the statute and HMRC's own manual. Schedule 10 paragraph 8(3)–(4), quoted verbatim:

"Where an amount of relevant foreign tax has been paid, or is payable, in respect of an amount of qualifying overseas capital, only so much of the amount as remains after deducting the amount of relevant foreign tax paid, or payable, may be designated…relevant foreign tax means a tax imposed by the law of a territory outside the United Kingdom that corresponds to (a) income tax, or (b) capital gains tax."

Indian TDS withheld on NRO interest under the Income-tax Act, 1961 and Indian capital gains tax paid on mutual fund or equity redemptions both fall squarely inside that definition. What that provision does, mechanically, is force the foreign tax out of the designated amount before the TRF rate is applied — it is a deduction from the base, not a credit against the charge. HMRC's RDRM73340 ("Designation process: Foreign tax credits") states the consequence without hedging, quoted verbatim in full:

"These amounts of designated qualifying overseas capital must be net of any foreign tax that has been paid or is payable. As such, foreign tax credit relief is not available against any qualifying overseas capital designated under the TRF. Where an individual chooses to designate an amount on which foreign tax has already been paid, the TRF rate will only apply to the net amount after foreign tax has been deducted. No further credit can be claimed against the TRF rate of 12% for 2025-26 and 2026-27 or 15% in the 2027-28 tax year. It will only be possible for an individual to claim a full credit for foreign tax paid overseas if they do not elect to designate the net amount under the TRF and instead remit the funds as an ordinary remittance."

The finding

TRF is a deduction mechanism, not a credit mechanism — Article 24 relief is foreclosed the moment you designate

Part 18 verified the India-UK DTAA (SI 1993/1801) Article 24(1)(a) credits Indian tax "against any United Kingdom tax computed by reference to the same profits, income or chargeable gains." The TRF charge is not computed that way. It is a flat rate applied to a capital sum from which any foreign tax already paid has been subtracted before the rate is applied — a deduction-of- base mechanism, structurally distinct from a credit-against-liability mechanism, and HMRC's own manual states plainly that once an amount is designated net-of-tax, "no further credit can be claimed." Schedule 10 itself, across its full text, never uses the words "treaty," "double tax," or "DTAA" — the deduction rule at paragraph 8(3)–(4) is drafted as a general anti-double-relief provision against any foreign tax, not as an implementation of, or interaction with, Article 24 specifically. The credit-vs-deduction outcome is HMRC guidance filling a genuine statutory silence, not the statute answering the treaty question directly — worth flagging as its own gap.

The practical consequence is a real, binary choice, and it cuts both ways depending on the reader's numbers. Designating under the TRF locks in 12–15% on the net-of-Indian-tax amount, permanently forfeiting the DTAA credit route for that money. Declining to designate and instead remitting the funds as an ordinary remittance keeps full access to Article 24 relief (credit for Indian tax against the corresponding UK liability) but means the income is taxed at the reader's full UK marginal income tax or CGT rate, not the flat TRF rate. Which side wins is a quantifiable question turning on the reader's applicable Indian tax rate relative to 12–15%: Indian NRO interest TDS typically runs around 30% (plus surcharge/cess) before any DTAA-reduced-rate certificate is applied, and Indian long-term capital gains tax on equity mutual funds runs at rates that, net of the TRF's 12% deduction-adjusted charge, can still leave TRF the cheaper route for many readers — but the comparison has to be run on the reader's actual net-of-Indian-tax amount and UK marginal rate, not assumed. This page states the mechanism, not a verdict on any individual's numbers.

Not resolved

Does India give any credit for the UK TRF charge, the other direction?

No HMRC guidance, tribunal decision, or professional commentary addressing this reverse direction was found this session. Reasoning from Indian foreign tax credit mechanics (Income-tax Act, 1961 ss.90/91 and Rule 128) rather than from any primary Indian-side text read this session: Indian credit computation generally requires the foreign tax to be identifiably "in respect of" specific income also taxed in India, and the TRF's own capital-based framing — Schedule 10 allows designating amounts that have already been used to acquire assets, not only amounts tied to a discrete, dated income event — would likely complicate that link. This is INFERENCE, reasoned from adjacent Indian statutory structure, not verified against Indian primary text or an Indian ruling this session, and is flagged as open rather than answered.

5. The window is time-limited — this is not evergreen guidance

Deadline

The TRF charge only exists for tax years 2025-26 through 2027-28

Per Schedule 10 paragraph 1(8), quoted above, the 12% rate applies only to amounts designated in a return for 2025-26 or 2026-27, and the 15% rate only to amounts designated for 2027-28. There is no rate specified beyond 2027-28 in the paragraph read this session — the facility, as drafted, simply does not extend past that year. A UK tax year runs 6 April to 5 April, so the final window to designate at the 15% rate is the 2027-28 tax year, ending 5 April 2028; each year's own designation deadline (a return due within 12 months of the following 31 January, per paragraph 8(1)) applies on top of that. Undesignated pre-2025 foreign income and gains that are remitted after the facility closes fall back to ordinary remittance-basis-transitional or current-law treatment — this page did not map that fallback regime in detail, since it sits outside what a reader inside the live window needs first. The point that matters here: every year a reader delays a designation decision is a year closer to losing the 12% rate (available only through 2026-27) and, eventually, the facility itself.

Questions worth putting to a UK-qualified accountant or tax adviser before designating anything

  1. What is the actual net-of-Indian-tax amount available to designate for each pot of pre-2025 Indian income or gains — TDS certificates and Indian capital gains computations will need to be pulled together first, since the TRF charge only applies to the amount left after that subtraction?
  2. Given that amount and this reader's expected UK marginal income tax or CGT rate, does 12% (or 15%, depending on the tax year of designation) on the net amount actually beat ordinary remittance plus the Article 24 credit — run as an explicit number, not assumed?
  3. For accounts that blend pre- and post-6-April-2025 Indian income, has the mixed-fund identification exercise (which slices qualify as "TRF capital" under the amended s.809Q Step A1) actually been done, or is a designation being made against an estimate?

Whether to designate any amount under the TRF, and how much, is an irreversible per-amount election with real money attached — once designated net-of-tax, HMRC's own manual states the credit route is gone for that money.
