Does Any Indian Pension Scheme Actually Pass HMRC's QROPS Transfer Test?

This is the third UK-vertical page, and it moves from Part 15's question — is Indian EPF an "overseas pension scheme" at all — to the stricter test that applies when someone actually wants to transfer a UK pension into an Indian scheme. Transfer-service marketing routinely names HDFC Life, ICICI Prudential, and "NPS transfer" in the same breath. HMRC's own live list, read in full this session, tells a narrower and more specific story than any of the marketing pages found.

New: SI 2006/206 Regulation 3 read in full at legislation.gov.uk · FA 2004 s.169 and HMRC PTM102200 (the 25% overseas transfer charge) read in full · HMRC's live ROPS notification list scanned for every Indian entry · five competitor/marketing pages read in full and checked against the primary text

Part 15 asked whether Indian EPF satisfies SI 2006/206 Regulation 2 — the definition of an "overseas pension scheme" that gates a UK lump-sum deduction under money already paid out. This page asks the separate, stricter question that matters to a different reader: someone who still has UK pension rights and is being marketed a transfer into an Indian scheme before those rights are ever paid out at all. The statutory gate for that is Regulation 3, and it sits on top of Regulation 2, not beside it.

1. Regulation 3 is not a separate test — it is Regulation 2 plus more

SI 2006/206 Regulation 3, read in full at legislation.gov.uk (current consolidated text, last amended 6 April 2025), opens with the structural relationship stated in the statute's own words, quoted verbatim:

"3.—(1) For the purposes of section 150(8) (recognised overseas pension schemes), in addition to satisfying the requirements set out in regulation 2 above, the pension scheme must—(a) except where it falls within paragraph (1A), satisfy the requirements in paragraphs (6) and (6A); and (b) satisfy…the requirement that the scheme must be established in a country or territory mentioned in paragraph (2)…"

That single phrase — "in addition to satisfying the requirements set out in regulation 2 above" — settles the structural question cleanly: a scheme must first clear Regulation 2's Regulation Requirements Test and Tax Recognition Test (the two tests Part 15 walked EPF through and found genuinely doubtful) before Regulation 3's own, additional conditions are even reached. Nothing in Regulation 3 relaxes Regulation 2. A scheme that fails Regulation 2 cannot become a Recognised Overseas Pension Scheme (ROPS, the current HMRC term; "QROPS" survives as the defined term in the underlying statute, FA 2004 s.169(1)(b)) by satisfying Regulation 3 alone.

Regulation 3's own additional conditions, quoted verbatim, are:

  • Paragraph (6), the non-discrimination rule: "Where tax relief in respect of benefits paid from the scheme is available to a member of the scheme who is not resident in the country or territory in which the scheme is established, the same or substantially the same tax relief must—(a) also be available to members of the scheme who are resident in the country or territory; and (b) apply regardless of whether the member was resident in the country or territory …when the member joined the scheme; or …for any period of time when they were a member of the scheme." This targets schemes that give favourable treatment specifically to attract non- resident (i.e. transferring-in) members — a scheme cannot be more generous to an incoming UK transferee than to its own resident members.
  • Paragraph (6A): benefit-payment timing must mirror UK pension-age rules for the transferred fund — broadly, no earlier access to the transferred portion than a UK registered scheme would allow, or the payment must be one that would count as an "authorised member payment" if made by a UK scheme.
  • Paragraph (2)/(1)(b)(i), the information-exchange condition: the scheme must be established in a country or territory covered by a UK information-exchange agreement under FA 2006 s.173 or TIOPA 2010 s.2. India has a long-standing bilateral tax treaty with the UK carrying an exchange-of-information article (see Part 14 and Part 15, section 5, for the treaty read in full); this specific Regulation 3 gate is very likely satisfied for India on that basis, though the EOI article's text was not independently re-read this session against this specific statutory cross-reference — flagged as a highly probable but not separately re-verified pass.

None of this is exotic drafting: it is standard anti-avoidance structure, closing off a scheme that would otherwise be attractive purely as a UK-transfer destination rather than on its own merits for local members. It is also, on its face, a test almost none of the marketing content found this session ever mentions by name.

2. The 25% overseas transfer charge — and the one exclusion condition every reader with an India move is actually asking about

Clearing Regulation 2 and Regulation 3 makes a scheme a ROPS. It does not, on its own, make a transfer to that scheme tax-free. Since Finance Act 2017, a separate income tax charge can apply to the transfer itself. HMRC's PTM102200 (Pensions Tax Manual, read in full), quoted verbatim:

"The overseas transfer charge is an income tax charge that applies to certain: recognised transfers from a registered pension scheme to a qualifying recognised overseas pension scheme (QROPS)…if the charge is under section 244AC, it will be 25% of the 'transferred value.'"

The charge does not apply if any one of five exclusion conditions is met. Quoted verbatim in full:

"The transferred value of a recognised transfer will be subject to an overseas transfer charge under section 244AC if none of the following exclusion conditions is met:
— The member is resident in the same country as that in which the QROPS receiving the transfer is established.
— The QROPS receiving the transfer is established in Gibraltar or a country within the European Economic Area (EEA) and the member is UK resident or resident in a country within the EEA or Gibraltar. Note that this exclusion applies only in respect of transfers requested before 30 October 2024 and completed before 30 April 2025.
— The QROPS is set up by an international organisation for the purpose of providing benefits for, or in respect of, past service as an employee of the organisation and the member is an employee of that international organisation…
— The QROPS is an overseas public service pension scheme and the member is an employee of an employer that participates in the scheme.
— The QROPS is an occupational pension scheme and the member is an employee of a sponsoring employer under the scheme."

Since the EEA exclusion is now time-barred for new transfers and does not cover India regardless, and the other three exclusions do not fit an ordinary individual moving pension savings on retirement, the first condition — "the member is resident in the same country as that in which the QROPS receiving the transfer is established" — is the one that actually governs the family pattern this page is about: a UK-resident Indian-origin saver moving back to India.

The genuinely open question

Whose residence test, and residence when?

PTM102200 states the residence-match condition but does not, on the page read this session, define what "resident" means for this purpose — whether it imports the UK's Statutory Residence Test, ordinary tax-residence concepts, or something else, and precisely which moment's residence controls. The page does state, and this is the point that directly addresses the timing distinction marketing tends to blur: the exclusion is tested at the time of the transfer, but is not final — HMRC separately operates a "change of circumstances" mechanism (referenced on this page as PTM102400, not read this session) that can retrospectively claw back an excluded transfer's tax-free status if the member's residence changes within a defined period after the transfer. That means the ordinary marketing framing — "transfer once you've moved, and you're fine" — is directionally right about the exclusion condition but silent on the clawback window, and this page did not read PTM102400 to establish that window's exact length or mechanics. Flagged as open, not resolved here.

What follows from the exclusion's plain wording, independent of that open point: a transfer initiated while still UK resident, before the move to India, does not meet the "resident in the same country as the QROPS" condition at the moment of transfer (the member is UK resident; the QROPS, if Indian, is established in India) — so, absent one of the other four narrow exclusions, the 25% charge is the default outcome for a transfer timed that way. A transfer initiated after the saver has already become India-resident and the QROPS is an Indian scheme meets the condition on its face. The sequencing marketing pages gesture at ("living in the same country as the QROPS you're transferring to") is the right general shape of the rule; none of the pages read this session spell out the transfer-then-clawback mechanics that make the timing question more than a one-off snapshot.

3. HMRC's live ROPS list: 35 Indian schemes, and every one of them is a commercial annuity product

HMRC's "Check the recognised overseas pension schemes notification list" page (gov.uk, last updated 1 September 2026 at the time this page was read) carries the following disclaimer, quoted verbatim in full:

"This list contains pension schemes that have told HMRC that they meet the conditions to be a recognised overseas pension scheme (ROPS) and have asked to be included on the list… We cannot guarantee these are ROPS or that any transfers to them will be free of UK tax. It's your responsibility to find out if you have to pay tax on any transfer of pension savings. We will usually pursue any UK tax charges (and interest for late payment) arising from transfers to overseas entities that do not meet the ROPS requirements even when they appear on this list. This includes where the ROPS requirements have changed and where taxpayers are overseas. We will also charge penalties in appropriate cases…You should seek suitable professional advice, including from a regulated financial adviser."

Two things about that quote matter for accuracy. First, it is exactly the disclaimer the underlying premise of this page's research expected to find, and it is exactly as blunt as expected — listing is self-notification, not HMRC approval, and the transferring member carries the downside if a listed scheme is later found not to have met the requirements, even retroactively. Second, and this needs to be stated as a correction rather than assumed: the current live disclaimer does not contain a specific "40%" tax-charge figure. A number in that range plausibly traces to the separate unauthorised-payment-charge regime (FA 2004 s.208 and related provisions, a different and older charge from the 25% overseas transfer charge under s.244AC discussed above), but that cross-reference was not independently verified against primary statutory text this session, and no archived version of the gov.uk page was checked to see whether an older disclaimer once stated a figure. This page states only what the current disclaimer actually says.

The list itself was read in full and scanned for every Indian entry. As of the 1 September 2026 update, 35 schemes are listed under India. Every one of them is a named commercial annuity or pension-insurance product sold by an IRDAI-regulated life insurer — not a government-administered retirement scheme, and not a generic "insurer" entry. The list includes named products from ABSLI, Axis Max Life, Bajaj Allianz/Bajaj Life, Canara HSBC, HDFC Life, ICICI Prudential Life, Kotak Life, LIC, PNB MetLife, SBI Life, and Tata AIA — for example "HDFC Life Guaranteed Pension Plan," "ICICI Pru Easy Retirement," "LIC's Jeevan Akshay — VII," each listed as its own separate scheme.

What is not on the list

Neither NPS nor EPF appears anywhere in HMRC's India section

The full India section of the live list was searched for "NPS," "National Pension," "EPF," "EPFO," and "PFRDA." None appear. Marketing content that speaks generically of "transferring your UK pension to NPS," or that names HDFC Life or ICICI Prudential as if the insurer's brand were itself the qualifying scheme, does not match what HMRC's list actually shows: qualification is granted (or rather, self-notified and published) at the level of one named product, not one company and not one scheme type in the abstract. A reader being pointed at "HDFC Life" or "an NPS transfer" cannot verify eligibility from that description alone — the specific product name has to appear on the list, and as of this reading, no NPS product does. This absence does not prove NPS could never qualify (the list is opt-in and self-notified, not an exhaustive statement of what is theoretically eligible) but it is a direct, verifiable data point: nobody has actually put an NPS-linked scheme on this list, in practice, as of September 2026.

4. The NPS-vs-EPF asymmetry: real on one test, and less clean than marketing implies on the other

Part 15 found Indian EPF plausibly fails Regulation 2's Tax Recognition Test because it is exempt at both ends — relief on contributions under s.80C, and exemption on withdrawal under ss.10(11)/10(12), the "EEE" structure. NPS is commonly described as EET rather than EEE, which raises the natural question this page's brief anticipated: does that difference make NPS pass where EPF fails?

INFERENCE — NPS tax treatment

NPS withdrawal is roughly 60% exempt, not fully exempt — but this was not confirmed against bare Income-tax Act text this session

Multiple current secondary sources, including HDFC Pension (an official NPS pension fund manager) and Protean (the NPS Central Recordkeeping Agency), consistently describe the position as: contribution relief is available under s.80CCD of the Income-tax Act, 1961, and at retirement only around 60% of the accumulated corpus can be withdrawn tax-free as a lump sum under s.10(12A), with the remainder either mandatorily annuitised (annuity income is separately taxable as it is received) or, if taken as a lump sum beyond the tax-free portion, potentially taxable. incometaxindia.gov.in's relevant pages could not be reached as a plain-text primary source this session (the site now serves this content through a JavaScript application that did not yield readable text via the tools used). This description is therefore INFERENCE, not verified against the bare statute the way EPF's s.80C/s.10(11)/s.10(12) treatment was verified in Part 15. It should be treated as probably right, not confirmed.

Taking that description at face value and running it against Regulation 2's Tax Recognition Condition 2 — quoted in full in Part 15 — produces a more nuanced result than a simple "NPS passes" headline. Condition 2 requires one of two alternative facts to be true: either "tax relief is not available to the member on contributions," or "all or most of the benefits paid by the scheme to members…are subject to taxation." NPS fails the first limb outright — contribution relief under s.80CCD plainly exists. And on the numbers reported above, NPS also does not clearly satisfy the second limb: if roughly 60% of the withdrawal is tax-free, that is not "all or most" of the benefit being taxed — it is a minority being taxed. On a literal reading of Condition 2's own wording, NPS may fail the Tax Recognition Test for essentially the same structural reason EPF does — neither limb of Condition 2 is squarely satisfied — even though NPS is unambiguously less generous, and taxed more, than EPF at the withdrawal stage. The marketed asymmetry (NPS transfers freely because it's "EET, not EEE") does not hold up cleanly against Condition 2's literal either/or wording; it is not confirmed to be wrong either, since no HMRC ruling addresses NPS by name and the "all or most" threshold is itself imprecise language this page cannot resolve to a bright line.

VERIFIED — the asymmetry that does hold up

NPS has an unambiguous external regulator; EPF's status is genuinely unclear

Read directly at pfrda.org.in: the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority was established by its own 2013 Act of Parliament (in force 1 February 2014) and sits outside and above the NPS's operational machinery — the NPS Trust, pension fund managers, custodian, trustee bank, and points of presence are all listed as separate entities that PFRDA itself regulates. This is a structurally different picture from EPF, where Part 15 found EPFO's own tripartite Central Board of Trustees both administers the scheme and sets its rules, with no clearly separate external regulator — the genuinely open question Part 15 left unresolved. On Regulation 2's Regulation Requirements Test (not the Tax Recognition Test discussed above), NPS very plausibly clears the "regulated by a body" branch in a way EPF's ambiguous position does not. The asymmetry between NPS and EPF is real, but it sits on the Regulation Requirements Test, not on the Tax Recognition Test the marketed "EET beats EEE" framing implicitly assumes.

Two further points this page flags but does not resolve. First, even a scheme that clears both Regulation 2 and Regulation 3 has to actually be willing and able, on the Indian side, to receive an inbound foreign pension transfer — whether NPS's own governing rules, PFRDA regulations, and India's foreign-exchange rules (FEMA) permit an NPS subscriber to receive a transfer-in from a UK pension scheme at all is a separate Indian-law question this page did not research and does not answer. Second, and consistent with section 3 above, no NPS-linked product currently appears on HMRC's ROPS list regardless of how the Regulation 2/3 analysis comes out on paper — the practical route, as of this reading, runs only through named commercial annuity products, not NPS.

A direct, checkable contradiction

One adviser-reviewed UK guide says the opposite of what HMRC's own list currently shows

QROP Direct's UK country guide for India, reviewed by "an independent regulated pension specialist" as recently as 11 June 2026, states plainly: "Indian pension schemes are not on the HMRC QROPS register. Both sides cite HMRC as their authority; only one of them appears to have actually checked the current list. This is not offered as a criticism of that guide's overall quality (its India tax-band and frozen State Pension content read as accurate and it carries proper regulatory framing) — it is offered as a concrete, quotable example of how quickly a twice-monthly-updated list can make even a careful, recently-reviewed guide wrong on the single fact that matters most.

A quotable omission

A page devoted entirely to "how to transfer your UK pension to India" that never mentions the transfer charge

Policybazaar's QROPS page markets named annuity products with monthly-income figures ("Tax Free Pension ₹1.12 L/month" in one example) as though the tax outcome of a transfer were simply settled and favourable. A full-text search of the extracted page (14,908 characters) for "transfer charge," "55%," "not guarantee," "risk," and "resident" (in the transfer-charge sense) returned zero matches for all of them. This is the clearest single divergence this session found between marketing content and the primary text: not a misstatement of the law, but a page whose entire purpose is explaining this transfer route while omitting every mechanism — the 25% charge, HMRC's own guarantee disclaimer, and the residence-timing condition — that could cost a reader money.

What none of the five pages do, without exception: distinguish a transfer initiated before the move to India from one initiated after, in the specific terms section 2 above sets out. That gap is consistent across marketing and adviser-reviewed content alike, and it is the mechanism most likely to catch a real reader by surprise — not because any page says something false about it, but because none of them say anything about it at all.

6. What is verified and what is not

  • VERIFIED — SI 2006/206 Regulation 3, read in full at legislation.gov.uk (current text, last amended 6 April 2025): Regulation 3 is cumulative on Regulation 2, not a standalone test, per its own opening words; its additional conditions (non-discrimination on benefits, payment-timing alignment, information-exchange country requirement) quoted verbatim above.
  • VERIFIED — FA 2004 s.169(1), read in full at legislation.gov.uk: the statutory definition of "recognised transfer," including transfers to "a qualifying recognised overseas pension scheme." Subsection (1) fully read; later subsections on annuity-purchase and scheme-pension continuity were not exhaustively pulled.
  • VERIFIED — HMRC PTM102200, read in full: the 25% overseas transfer charge under s.244AC, all five exclusion conditions quoted verbatim, and the fact that the exclusion is tested at the time of transfer but is subject to a later "change of circumstances" clawback mechanism (PTM102400, not itself read this session).
  • VERIFIED — HMRC's live ROPS notification list (gov.uk, updated 1 September 2026), disclaimer quoted verbatim in full, and its India section: 35 named schemes, all commercial insurer annuity/pension products, zero NPS or EPF entries.
  • VERIFIED — PFRDA's status as NPS's external statutory regulator, read directly at pfrda.org.in.
  • INFERENCE — NPS's tax treatment (roughly 60% tax-free lump sum under s.10(12A), balance taxable or mandatorily annuitised), from multiple consistent secondary sources; not confirmed against bare Income-tax Act text this session.
  • Not resolved — the precise residence test (whose rules, and exactly which moment) that governs PTM102200's first exclusion condition; the exact mechanics and time window of the "change of circumstances" clawback (PTM102400); whether NPS's own governing rules and India's foreign-exchange law would even permit an inbound transfer from a UK scheme; whether a "40%" tax- charge figure appears anywhere in HMRC's own current published material (not found on the live disclaimer page read this session) or only in the separate, older unauthorised-payment-charge regime.

Questions worth putting to a UK-qualified, FCA-authorised pension transfer adviser before relying on any of this

  1. Is the specific receiving scheme — not just the insurer's brand name, not "NPS" generically — currently on HMRC's live ROPS notification list, checked on the date closest to the actual transfer (the list changes twice monthly)?
  2. Does the transfer's timing, relative to the saver's actual change of tax residence, satisfy PTM102200's residence-match exclusion, and what does the "change of circumstances" clawback mean for a transfer completed shortly before or after the move?
  3. If the receiving scheme is a commercial annuity product rather than NPS or EPF, what happens to that product's own India-side tax treatment, and does it change anything about the UK-side analysis in Part 15's OPS framework?
This is FCA-regulated advice territory

Transferring a UK pension is not a decision this page, or any reading of primary sources, can make for you

In the UK, advising on the transfer of pension rights with safeguarded benefits (broadly, defined- benefit and similar schemes) above £30,000 in value is a regulated activity that legally requires advice from an FCA-authorised pension transfer specialist. None of the five marketing or adviser pages read this session state that requirement with equal prominence, and this page does not attempt to substitute for it. What is above is a reading of statutes, HMRC's own published manual and notification list, and named marketing pages — nothing here is a recommendation to transfer, or not to transfer, any pension.

This is not tax or financial advice, and it is not pension transfer advice. It is a reading of one set of regulations, one HMRC manual, one live government list, and five named marketing/adviser pages, run condition-by-condition so that the right questions can be asked before anyone relies on a paraphrase — including this one. UK pension transfer advice is a regulated professional service, and for schemes with safeguarded benefits above £30,000 it is a legal requirement, not merely a good idea: take advice from a UK FCA-authorised pension transfer specialist on your specific facts before acting. It creates no professional relationship.
