Canada's departure tax and Indian EPF/PPF: what "excluded right or interest" actually covers

This is the second Canada-vertical page in this series: the reader is a Canadian tax resident with an Indian EPF or PPF balance who is leaving Canada — moving back to India, or onward to a third country. Section 128.1(4) deems almost everything you own sold at fair market value the day you cease residency. There is a pension carve-out. Whether it reaches an Indian provident fund depends on which of eleven separate limbs you test it against — and this page finds that EPF and PPF plausibly land on opposite sides of that test, for a reason no guide read this session mentions and that sits in real tension with this series' own prior finding on the same statute.

New Canada-vertical page · Sources read in full · Income Tax Act s.128.1(4), s.128.1(10) & Income Tax Regulation 6802 (laws-lois.justice.gc.ca) · CRA's own "Dispositions of property for emigrants of Canada" guide (canada.ca) · five competitor guides read in full, quoted where relevant · cross-references Part 16's Regulation 6803 finding

Roughly 1.86 million people of Indian origin live in Canada (2021 Census, StatCan) — the same population this series cited when it opened the Canada vertical in Part 16. That page asked whether EPF/PPF has to be reported on Form T1135 while you hold it. This page asks the mirror question: what happens to it the day you stop being a Canadian tax resident, before you have withdrawn a rupee of it. The two questions turn out to use two different statutory tests, with two different answers — and reading both tests against the same fund is what this page does that no competitor guide, including the strongest one, does.

1. The baseline: departure tax catches EPF/PPF unless something excludes it

Income Tax Act s.128.1(4), read in full at laws-lois.justice.gc.ca, deems a taxpayer who ceases to be a Canadian resident to have disposed of "each property owned by the taxpayer" at fair market value immediately before departure, with four exceptions for individuals. Quoted verbatim, paragraph (b):

"the taxpayer is deemed to have disposed… of each property owned by the taxpayer other than, if the taxpayer is an individual, (i) real or immovable property situated in Canada, a Canadian resource property or a timber resource property, (ii) capital property used in… a business carried on by the taxpayer through a permanent establishment… in Canada…, (iii) an excluded right or interest of the taxpayer, (iv) if the taxpayer is not a trust and was not, during the 120-month period that ends at the particular time, resident in Canada for more than 60 months, property that was owned by the taxpayer at the time the taxpayer last became resident in Canada…"

An Indian EPF or PPF balance is not Canadian real property and not Canadian business property. The 60-month exception (iv) only helps someone who was Canadian-resident for five years or less in the decade before leaving — a real carve-out for recent arrivals, but not for anyone who spent longer in Canada. That leaves exactly one door: is EPF or PPF an "excluded right or interest" under s.128.1(10)? If not, the fund is deemed disposed of and reacquired at fair market value on departure day, and any accrued gain since the last valuation event enters the departure-year Canadian return — the exact mechanic Part 16 already verified for the arrival-side mirror image, s.128.1(1).

2. The definition has eleven limbs. Two look relevant. Neither is a clean yes.

Subsection 128.1(10), read in full, defines "excluded right or interest" as a list of eleven paragraphs, (a) through (l), most of them Canadian-specific (RRSPs, RRIFs, TFSAs, the Canada Pension Plan). Two sub-items are the only ones that could plausibly reach a foreign scheme like EPF or PPF, and this page tests both against the actual features of each fund rather than assuming either applies.

2a. Paragraph (a)(x): "a foreign retirement arrangement" — already closed by Part 16

Paragraph (a) of the definition includes, among rights under Canadian registered plans:

"(x) a foreign retirement arrangement…"

This term is defined at s.248(1) as "a prescribed plan or arrangement," and the only prescription is Income Tax Regulation 6803, which Part 16 already read in full and quoted verbatim: a closed list naming only plans governed by IRC §408(a), (b) or (h) — US Individual Retirement Accounts, nothing else. EPF and PPF cannot reach this limb for the same reason they cannot reach the T1135 "exempt trust" carve-out that turns on the identical defined term. This door is shut, and it was shut by Part 16's own reading, not a new finding here.

2b. Paragraph (a)(viii): "a superannuation or pension fund or plan" — undefined, and this series has already been skeptical of calling EPF a "pension"

Paragraph (a)(viii) covers:

"a superannuation or pension fund or plan (other than an employee benefit plan)…"

Unlike "foreign retirement arrangement," this phrase is not sent to a regulation and has no statutory definition anywhere in the Act. On its face it is broad enough to read as country-agnostic. But Part 8 of this series, examining a different classification question (whether an EPF lump sum is a treaty "pension" under the US-India DTAA), found the Treasury's own Technical Explanation treating a one-time provident-fund withdrawal as distinct from a periodic pension payment. EPF is structurally a defined- contribution account with an individual balance, not a pooled annuity-style benefit — the same feature that made the "pension" label uncomfortable in Part 8. This page does not lean on (a)(viii); it is flagged here as the natural first guess and set aside as too weak to carry the argument, in favour of a sturdier limb below.

2c. Paragraph (g)(iv): "a plan or arrangement instituted by the social security legislation of a country other than Canada" — the limb no competitor tests

Paragraph (g) of the definition covers a right to a benefit under several social-insurance programs. Quoted verbatim, in full:

"(g) a right of the individual to a benefit under (i) the Canada Pension Plan or a provincial pension plan…, (ii) the Old Age Security Act, or… (iv) a plan or arrangement instituted by the social security legislation of a country other than Canada or of a state, province or other political subdivision of such a country;"

This sub-paragraph is not sent to any regulation — it stands on its own words in the Act. It is also not unique drafting: Income Tax Regulation 6802, read in full at laws-lois.justice.gc.ca, uses near-identical language ("a plan or arrangement instituted by the social security legislation of a country other than Canada…") for a different, unrelated purpose — excluding such plans from the definition of "retirement compensation arrangement" at s.248(1)(n). The repetition across two separate provisions is not itself authority for EPF, but it shows this is a recognized statutory category, not an ad hoc reading of five words.

The classification question this page turns on

Is EPF "instituted by the social security legislation" of India?

The Employees' Provident Fund is created and administered under the Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952. That Act was consolidated in 2020 into India's own omnibus Code on Social Security, 2020, which explicitly folds the EPF Act into India's social-security legislative architecture — confirmed by India's own Ministry of Labour & Employment materials and by PwC's Worldwide Tax Summaries country note for India, which states plainly: "Indian social security is broadly governed by the Employees' Provident Fund and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952 (PF Act) and schemes made thereunder." That is an independent, professional characterization of EPF's home statute as India's social security legislation, not this page's own label. On that reading, an EPF balance plausibly is "a plan… instituted by the social security legislation of… India" within the plain words of paragraph (g)(iv) — a limb with no US-only regulation narrowing it the way Regulation 6803 narrows paragraph (a)(x).

PPF does not have the same institutional footing. The Public Provident Fund is governed by the Government Savings Promotion Act, 1873 (the former Government Savings Banks Act) and the Public Provident Fund Scheme, 2019 notified under it — a small-savings statute administered by India's Department of Economic Affairs, open to any resident regardless of employment, sitting alongside instruments like the Senior Citizens' Savings Scheme. It is not part of the Code on Social Security, 2020 and is not administered by India's labour/social-security apparatus (EPFO) the way EPF is. A PPF account reads far more naturally as a government-backed savings instrument than as a "plan or arrangement instituted by social security legislation." EPF and PPF plausibly sit on opposite sides of the same statutory test — a distinction that matters because popular guidance and this series' own earlier pages have sometimes treated EPF and PPF as an interchangeable pair for tax purposes when the underlying statutes that create them are not equivalent.

3. The tension with Part 16's own finding, stated honestly

Part 16 established, reading Regulation 6803 verbatim, that EPF and PPF cannot qualify as a "foreign retirement arrangement" for T1135 exempt-trust purposes because the only prescribed plans are US IRC §408 accounts. This page does not disturb that finding — it remains correct on its own terms, for its own purpose (whether the fund is specified foreign property that must be reported each year it is held). What this page adds is that departure tax's "excluded right or interest" definition is not the same test. It does not route through "foreign retirement arrangement" or Regulation 6803 at all for the (g)(iv) limb; it uses an entirely separate, unregulated phrase or its own words in the Act. A fund can fail one test and plausibly pass the other, because they are different statutory questions asking different things, in different words, for different purposes (ongoing annual reporting versus a one-time deemed-disposition event). That is not a contradiction in the Act and it is not a contradiction in Part 16's reasoning — it is two genuinely different provisions that happen to sound similar because both involve the word "pension" or "retirement" somewhere in their vicinity. The honest, unsettled finding is the tension itself: a Canadian resident with EPF could plausibly owe nothing on departure (if (g)(iv) applies) while still having had to report the same fund on T1135 every year they held it (because (a)(x)/Regulation 6803 does not apply) — two different answers to two different questions about the identical asset, and no guide read this session walks a reader through why both can be true at once.

4. What five competitor guides say — and mostly don't say

Search terms used: "leaving Canada departure tax EPF," "moving back to India from Canada taxes PPF," "emigrating Canada Indian assets deemed disposition." Five pages were read in full.

TrustNRI's dedicated departure-tax page (situations/canada-departure-tax-deemed-disposition-indian-assets), the strongest and most specific competitor content found, covers Indian real estate and Indian shares/mutual funds/gold in detail, with a worked example and the subsection 126(2.21) foreign-tax-credit mechanism for the resulting double-tax overlap with India's own capital-gains rules. Its own asset table states, quoted verbatim:

"RRSP, RRIF, TFSA, pensions — No, excluded"

EPF and PPF are not mentioned anywhere on this page, in the table or the text — not as caught, not as excluded, not at all.

TrustNRI's separate departure-tax blog post (blog/canada-nri-departure-tax-section-128-1) lists what is exempted from the deeming as "RRSP, RRIF, RPP, TFSA… Canadian-source pensions" and what is caught as "non-RRSP brokerage holdings… cryptocurrency… Indian shares held directly through a Canadian-resident broker… foreign rental property… precious metals." Again, no EPF, no PPF, no mention of a foreign provident fund or foreign social-security scheme in either list.

TrustNRI's dedicated EPF/NPS pension page (situations/canada-indian-pension-epf-nps-tax), read in Part 16 and re-checked here, covers Article 18 treaty taxation of an EPF withdrawal and its T1135 status in detail — but says nothing about what happens to an unwithdrawn EPF balance on the day a reader ceases Canadian residency. Three separate TrustNRI pages, the strongest specialist content available, and the departure-tax question for this specific asset is untouched across all three.

returntoindia.blog's "Indians Leaving Canada" guide, a long, sourced, cross-referenced piece (13-minute read, seven cited official sources, updated August 2026) with a detailed asset-by-asset table covering permanent residence, tax residency, non-registered investments, RRSP, TFSA, CPP, OAS and provincial health coverage, does not mention EPF, PPF, or any Indian retirement instrument anywhere in its text, table, or FAQ.

Dinesh Aarjav & Associates' Canada page, from an established India-based CA firm serving NRI clients across 25+ countries, lists "Indian shares, mutual funds, PF, LICs" under T1135 reporting but describes departure tax only generically as "Deemed Disposition of worldwide assets… Reporting exit from RRSP, TFSA, or other registered plans," again without naming EPF, PPF, or addressing whether a foreign provident fund could be excluded.

The absence, stated plainly

Five pages, three from the strongest specialist in the space, zero mentions

This is not a case of competitors getting the classification wrong the way Part 16 found on Article 18 — it is a case of the question simply not being asked. Departure tax is clearly a live, well-served topic (TrustNRI alone has three dedicated pages on adjacent angles of it); the specific sub-question of what happens to an Indian provident-fund balance under the "excluded right or interest" definition, and specifically whether paragraph (g)(iv)'s social-security-legislation limb reaches it, does not appear to have been asked in public by any guide read this session. CRA's own official guidance has the same gap: its "Dispositions of property for emigrants of Canada" page, read in full at canada.ca, paraphrases the excluded categories as "pension plans, annuities… employee benefit plans… rights or interests in certain other trusts" and directs readers to "the definition… in subsection 128.1(10)… for a complete list" without naming any foreign-scheme limb specifically — an honest, non-misleading gap (CRA is not required to enumerate every paragraph), but one that leaves a reader with no reason to go and check paragraph (g)(iv) at all.

5. The deferral election works either way

Whatever the answer to the classification question, a reader who concludes EPF or PPF is caught by the deemed disposition is not without options. Form T1244, under s.220(4.5), lets a departing resident elect to defer payment of the resulting tax until the property is actually disposed of — CRA's own guide, read in full, confirms this "applies, regardless of the amount," subject to posting security if the deferred federal tax exceeds roughly CAD 16,500. This does not resolve the classification question; it only changes when the tax, if owed at all, becomes payable. TrustNRI's departure-tax pages cover this mechanism accurately for the assets they do discuss (Indian real estate, shares), and the same form and election would apply to an EPF or PPF balance found to be caught.

6. What is verified and what is not

  • VERIFIED — Income Tax Act s.128.1(4)(b), read verbatim at laws-lois.justice.gc.ca: departure deems disposition of "each property," with four exceptions for individuals, of which "excluded right or interest" is the only one that could apply to a foreign provident fund held longer than the 60-month newcomer window.
  • VERIFIED — Income Tax Act s.128.1(10), read verbatim: the "excluded right or interest" definition has eleven paragraphs; paragraph (a)(x) ("foreign retirement arrangement") is closed to EPF/PPF by Part 16's already-verified reading of Regulation 6803 (US IRC §408 plans only); paragraph (a)(viii) ("superannuation or pension fund or plan") is undefined and, per Part 8's prior finding on a different classification question, not a comfortable fit for a defined-contribution provident-fund account.
  • VERIFIED — paragraph (g)(iv) ("a plan or arrangement instituted by the social security legislation of a country other than Canada"), read verbatim, is not sent to any regulation and uses language echoed independently in Income Tax Regulation 6802 for a separate purpose (retirement compensation arrangements).
  • VERIFIED — EPF's home statute, the Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952, is folded into India's Code on Social Security, 2020, and is characterized by PwC's Worldwide Tax Summaries as the legislation that "broadly governs" Indian social security. PPF's home statute is the Government Savings Promotion Act, 1873, a small-savings statute unconnected to India's social-security legislative architecture.
  • INFERENCE, re-checked 2026-09-03, narrowed — that EPF (not PPF) therefore qualifies as an "excluded right or interest" under paragraph (g)(iv) and escapes departure tax. No CRA technical interpretation, ruling, or Tax Court decision applying paragraph (g)(iv) itself, or Reg 6802(g)'s identical phrase, to EPF or any departure-tax fact pattern was found this session either (taxinterpretations.com and CanLII searched directly: "social security legislation of a country other than Canada", "128.1(10)(g)" OR "6802(g)", and India/Singapore/Germany-specific variants). But a related CRA interpretation was found and changes the weight of evidence: 11 July 2002 Internal T.I. 2002-0148267 ("Article 29 of the Canada-Germany Tax Treaty"), read at taxinterpretations.com, applies Reg 6802(g)'s identical wording (in the parallel context of the retirement-compensation-arrangement definition) and states the exclusion "is meant to exclude benefits accruing in plans created by the governments of countries other than Canada, under their social security systems" — framing CRA itself has not applied to EPF, but whose emphasis on government-created, social-security-system plans reads more naturally toward pooled, pay-as-you-go programs (CPP/OAS analogues) than an individually-funded defined-contribution account. EPF sits in an ambiguous middle CRA's own language does not resolve: it is government-created and statutorily administered (by EPFO under the 1952 Act), which cuts toward the finding, but individually funded and owned, which cuts away from it. Net effect: the statutory fit remains plausible and, on the EPF/PPF asymmetry specifically, well-supported by each fund's actual home legislation, but this session's search turned up a reason for caution rather than a reason to upgrade — the tag stays INFERENCE, now resting on one adjacent, non-EPF CRA interpretation rather than none.
  • VERIFIED — five competitor guides read in full (three from TrustNRI, the strongest specialist found, plus returntoindia.blog and Dinesh Aarjav & Associates) do not address whether EPF or PPF is caught by or excluded from Canada's departure tax, despite each covering departure tax and/or EPF/T1135 separately in detail. CRA's own official emigrant-property guide paraphrases the excluded-right-or-interest list without naming the social-security-legislation limb.
  • Not resolved — whether a lump-sum, individually-owned provident fund like EPF falls within "social security legislation" in the sense Parliament intended by that phrase, versus only pooled state pension/insurance programs. This is the genuine open question this page raises rather than closes; see Section 3 for why it can coexist, without contradiction, alongside Part 16's opposite-facing finding on the T1135 test for the identical asset.

Questions worth putting to a CPA or Canadian tax lawyer before filing a departure-year return

  1. Does my EPF balance qualify as "a plan or arrangement instituted by the social security legislation" of India under s.128.1(10)(g)(iv) — and on what authority, given no CRA ruling naming EPF specifically was found in this research?
  2. Does my PPF balance qualify for any limb of "excluded right or interest," given its home statute (the Government Savings Promotion Act, 1873) is not social-security legislation in the same sense as the EPF Act?
  3. If either fund is caught by the deemed disposition, what is its fair market value on my departure date, and should I file Form T1244 to defer the resulting tax until actual withdrawal?
  4. How does the answer here interact with the Form T1135 reporting obligation Part 16 already established applies regardless — is there a risk of appearing inconsistent to CRA by treating the same fund as both reportable (T1135) and excluded (departure tax) in the same filing history?
