India’s 20% LRS tax collection isn’t automatically a US foreign tax credit

Every Indian guide to TCS on outward remittances calls it “creditable.” That is true — against your Indian tax bill. Whether it survives as a US foreign tax credit is a separate question, governed by a separate regulation, and nobody who covers TCS in depth appears to have asked it.

Sources read in full · 26 CFR 1.901-2 · Income-tax Act, 2025, §394 · RBI Master Direction on the Liberalised Remittance Scheme

This page is for a narrower audience than the rest of this series: a US citizen or green-card holder who is also an Indian tax resident — filing both an Indian ITR and a US Form 1040 for the same year — and who remits money abroad under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS). The Indian-side mechanics of the 20% Tax Collected at Source are covered thoroughly, and correctly, by several detailed guides. What follows is the question none of them ask: does the amount collected qualify as a foreign income tax paid, under the US Treasury’s own definition, for the year it was collected?

The one most often missed

1. A refundable amount is not a “foreign income tax paid” — by the regulation’s own words

TCS on LRS is not a final tax. It is a prepayment, collected by the bank on the full remitted amount and later set off against the remitter’s actual Indian income-tax liability for the year. If the remitter’s real Indian tax bill turns out to be smaller than the TCS collected — a routine outcome when the remitted money is savings or principal rather than income — the excess is refunded, with interest, on filing the Indian return. That is exactly the fact pattern the US foreign tax credit regulation addresses directly:

26 CFR 1.901-2(e)(2)(i), Refundable amounts: “An amount remitted to a foreign country is not an amount of foreign income tax paid to the extent that it is reasonably certain that the amount will be refunded, rebated, abated, or forgiven. It is reasonably certain that an amount will be refunded, rebated, abated, or forgiven to the extent the amount exceeds a reasonable approximation of final foreign income tax liability to the foreign country.”

Read plainly: the part of the TCS that gets refunded in India was never a creditable US foreign tax in the first place, no matter how the Indian return characterises it. A dual filer who claims the full 20% as a dollar-for-dollar Section 901 credit — because every Indian-facing article calls TCS “fully creditable” — is claiming a credit for money the Indian government is going to send back.

2. TCS is collected on the remittance, not on income — which is exactly why the refund case is common, not exceptional

The statute is unambiguous about what triggers collection: the act of remitting money abroad, not the presence of taxable income in that remittance.

Income-tax Act, 2025, §394(1), Table Sl. No. 7 (successor to the former §206C(1G) of the Income-tax Act, 1961): “Remittance under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme of an amount or aggregate of the amounts exceeding ten lakh rupees” — collected by the “Authorised dealer” at “20% for purposes other than education or medical treatment.”

Nothing in that table asks whether the remitted money is income at all. A resident who remits ten years of accumulated, already-taxed savings to fund an overseas property purchase has TCS collected on the full transferred amount, even though little or none of it represents current-year income with a matching current-year Indian tax liability. The RBI’s own Master Direction on the Scheme confirms the plumbing: it caps total outward remittance at USD 2,50,000 per financial year per resident individual, requires a PAN on every Form A2, and places no requirement that the remitted sum be income rather than capital — the Liberalised Remittance Scheme is a foreign-exchange control, not an income concept. That gap between what triggers TCS (the remittance) and what a final Indian tax bill is computed on (net taxable income) is precisely the gap that produces a refund, and precisely the gap that Rev. Proc.-style Indian commentary does not carry over into the US analysis.

A second, unresolved layer

3. Even the non-refunded portion needs a foreign-source income category to attach to

Section 901 credits are not a single pool — Section 904 requires foreign tax to be matched against foreign-source income within the correct limitation category before it can actually offset US tax. TCS is assessed against the remitted amount as a whole and only later netted against the remitter’s aggregate Indian liability; it is not tagged, at the point of collection, to any specific item of foreign-source income. Whether — and how — a preparer allocates the non-refunded portion of TCS to a Section 904 category was not resolved by reading Reg. 1.901-2 alone; the statute and its own sourcing rules were not read in full this pass. This is flagged as an open question, not answered here. A remitter funding a purchase from already-taxed principal may find that even the surviving, non-refunded slice of TCS has no foreign-source income to attach to at all.

4. What the strongest India-side guides get right — and where they stop

The best LRS/TCS explainers found are detailed and accurate on India’s own rules: the rate history across the 2020, 2023 and 2024 Finance Acts, the Rs 10 lakh threshold, the Form 26AS/AIS reconciliation trail, and the Form 12BAA mechanism that lets a salaried employee recover TCS through reduced monthly withholding instead of waiting for a refund the following year. Several of them use the word “creditable” prominently, and one even discusses Form 67 (the mechanism for claiming an Indian credit for tax paid abroad) on the same page as TCS. None of them run the reverse direction: whether TCS collected in India survives as a US Section 901 credit for a remitter who also files in the US. That is not a gap in their competence — it is simply outside the audience they are writing for. It is, however, exactly the audience this page is for.

What this changes in practice

  • Before claiming TCS as a US foreign tax credit, compare the TCS collected against the remitter’s actual, final Indian tax liability for that year — not the gross amount collected at the bank. Only the portion that is not reasonably certain to be refunded is a candidate for Section 901 treatment.
  • A remittance funded from savings, inheritance, or other already-taxed principal is the highest-risk case for over-claiming: TCS is collected in full regardless of whether the money represents current-year income, so the refundable share can be large.
  • Form 12BAA changes the timing of TCS recovery in India (through reduced salary withholding rather than a later refund) but does not change this analysis — the amount recovered through Form 12BAA is, by definition, not part of final Indian tax liability either.
  • This page does not resolve the Section 904 basket-allocation question in (3). Treat that as open, not as settled in either direction.

Questions worth putting to a preparer

  1. Are you claiming the gross TCS collected as a foreign tax credit, or only the portion that survives after netting against final Indian tax liability for the year?
  2. Has the source of the remitted funds been checked — is this a remittance of income, or of already-taxed principal that will generate little Indian tax and a large refund?
  3. If Form 12BAA was used to recover TCS through reduced salary withholding, has that recovered amount been excluded from the US credit claim?
  4. What foreign-source income, and what Section 904 category, is the surviving TCS being matched against?
  5. Is your position supported by a reading of Reg. 1.901-2 itself, or by the assumption that whatever India calls “creditable” is automatically creditable in the US too?
