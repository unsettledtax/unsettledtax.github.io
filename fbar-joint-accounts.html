The joint account you didn’t know you owned, for FBAR purposes

Being named on a parent’s Indian bank account puts a US person on the hook for FBAR — whose money it is has nothing to do with it. Read against 31 CFR 1010.350 directly, not the summaries.

Sources read in full · 31 CFR 1010.350 · IRS FBAR resource page · FinCEN Form 114a

Almost every US-based FBAR guide aimed at Indian families gets the basic rule right: foreign accounts over the threshold must be reported. Fewer get the joint-account mechanics right, because the regulation’s actual test — who is the owner of record — is easy to misread as a question about whose money is in the account. It isn’t. What follows is what 31 CFR 1010.350 actually says, read directly rather than through a summary.

The one most often missed

1. Being named on the account is the whole test — source of funds is irrelevant

The common assumption: “It’s my father’s NRE account. I’m only on it so the bank doesn’t freeze it if something happens to him. I’ve never put money in or taken money out.” None of that matters under the regulation.

31 CFR 1010.350(e)(1), Financial interest — owner of record: “A United States person has a financial interest in each bank, securities, or other financial account in a foreign country for which… the United States person is the owner of record or holder of legal title, whether the account is maintained for his or her own benefit or for the benefit of others including non-United States persons.”

Being listed as a joint holder makes you the “owner of record.” The clause “whether…for his or her own benefit or for the benefit of others” closes off the exact argument most people reach for. There is no economic-interest test, no source-of-funds test, and no exception for accounts opened purely for succession convenience. Section (a) sets two independent triggers — “financial interest in, or signature or other authority over” — so satisfying either one alone is enough.

2. The spousal joint-account exception does not reach a parent-child account

FBAR guidance correctly tells married couples they can skip a duplicate filing. What gets lost is how narrow that exception is, and that it has no parent-child, sibling, or adult-child-with-parent equivalent.

IRS, Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR): “You don’t need to file an FBAR for the calendar year if… You jointly own all your foreign financial accounts with your spouse and: You completed and signed FinCEN Form 114a authorizing your spouse to file on your behalf, and your spouse reports the jointly owned accounts on a timely-filed signed FBAR.”

Read closely, this exception has three conditions that a parent-child account fails on the first one: the co-owner must be a spouse. An NRI parent is not a spouse, and in the ordinary case is not even a US person, so there is no possibility of designating them as the filer under Form 114a in the first place. Every US-person child named on an Indian parent’s account must file their own FBAR every year the account exceeds the threshold — there is no shortcut.

3. The narrow exceptions in the regulation are aimed at bank employees, not families

31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2) does carve out several situations where signature authority does not require reporting. Reading the actual list makes clear who they are for:

“An officer or employee of a bank that is examined by the [OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC, OTS, or NCUA] need not report… if the officer or employee has no personal financial interest in the account…” — plus parallel carve-outs for officers of SEC/CFTC-registered entities, US military banking facilities, and bank-to-bank correspondent/nostro accounts.

Every exception in the subsection is occupational — a bank officer, a regulated entity’s employee, a military installation, an interbank settlement account. None of them contemplate an ordinary family member added to a personal account for convenience. A plain reading finds nothing in (f)(2) that reaches the family case, which means the general rule in (a) and (e)(1) applies without modification.

Reported, not confirmed against the primary instructions

4. Each joint owner reports the full account value, not a pro-rata share

Every FBAR-preparer site checked states that a joint owner must report the account’s full maximum value during the year, not a fractional share reflecting actual ownership or contribution. That is consistent with how FinCEN Form 114 is structured — it asks for “maximum value of account during calendar year,” with no field for a co-owner's percentage — but the FinCEN line-item filing instructions PDF that states this most directly could not be rendered as text during this research pass (only the search-indexed secondary sources were confirmed). Treat this specific point as well-corroborated but not independently read from FinCEN's own instructions in this pass — a reasonable next step before relying on it for a filing.

What this changes in practice

  • An NRE or NRO account opened jointly with a US-citizen or green-card-holder child, for succession planning only, is reportable by that child — every year, at the full account value — regardless of who funded it or who uses it.
  • This is a per-account, per-person test. A family with three Indian accounts held jointly across two US-person children can produce six separate reporting obligations, not two.
  • FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) and Form 8938 (FATCA) use different definitions of reportable interest and different thresholds — passing one test says nothing about the other. This page addresses FBAR only.
  • None of this turns on NRE vs. NRO account type. The distinction matters for Indian tax and repatriability, not for whether the account is "foreign" under 31 CFR 1010.350.

Questions worth putting to a preparer

  1. Am I named as a joint owner, or do I only have signature authority? The regulation treats these as two independent triggers — check both, not just one.
  2. Have you confirmed no 31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2) exception applies, rather than assuming family accounts are covered by some general carve-out? Read the list — it names specific occupations, not relationships.
  3. Is every account being reported at its own full maximum value, or has a pro-rata share been assumed anywhere?
  4. Are FBAR and Form 8938 being assessed as separate tests with separate thresholds, rather than treating an FBAR filing as if it also resolves 8938?
